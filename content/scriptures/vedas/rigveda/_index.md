The ***Rigveda*** or ***Rig Veda*** ([Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit_language "Sanskrit language"): [ऋग्वेद](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E0%A4%8B%E0%A4%97%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%B5%E0%A5%87%E0%A4%A6 "wikt:ऋग्वेद"), [IAST](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IAST "IAST"): *ṛgveda*, from [ऋच्](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E0%A4%8B%E0%A4%9A%E0%A5%8D "wikt:ऋच्"), "praise" [^15] and [वेद](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E0%A4%B5%E0%A5%87%E0%A4%A6 "wikt:वेद"), "knowledge") is an [ancient Indian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Indian "Ancient Indian") [collection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miscellany "Miscellany") of [Vedic Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedic_Sanskrit "Vedic Sanskrit") [hymns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hymn "Hymn") (*sūktas*). It is one of the four sacred canonical [Hindu texts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_texts "Hindu texts") (*[śruti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%9Aruti "Śruti")*) known as the [Vedas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedas "Vedas").[^16] [^17] Only one [Shakha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakha "Shakha") of the many survive today, namely the [Śakalya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakala_Shakha "Shakala Shakha") Shakha. Much of the contents contained in the remaining Shakhas are now lost or are not available in the public forum.[^18]

The *Rigveda* is the oldest known [Vedic Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedic_Sanskrit "Vedic Sanskrit") text.[^19] Its early layers are among the oldest extant texts in any [Indo-European language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-European_language "Indo-European language").[^20] [^2] Most scholars believe that the sounds and texts of the *Rigveda* have been orally transmitted with precision since the 2nd millennium BCE,[^22] [^23] [^24] through [methods of memorisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_mathematics#Styles_of_memorisation "Indian mathematics") of exceptional complexity, rigour and fidelity,[^25] [^26] [^27] though the dates are not confirmed and remain contentious till concrete evidence surfaces.[^28] [Philological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philological "Philological") and [linguistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_linguistics "Historical linguistics") evidence indicates that the bulk of the *Rigveda* Samhita was composed in the northwestern region of the [Indian subcontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_subcontinent "Indian subcontinent") (see [Rigvedic rivers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigvedic_rivers "Rigvedic rivers")), most likely between c. 1500 and 1000 BCE,[^29] [^30] [^31] although a wider approximation of c. 1900–1200 BCE has also been given.[^32] [^33] [^1]

The text is layered, consisting of the *Samhita*, *Brahmanas*, *Aranyakas* and *Upanishads*.[^3] The *Rigveda Samhita* is the core text and is a collection of 10 books (**maṇḍala** s) with 1,028 hymns (**sūkta** s) in about 10,600 verses (called **ṛc**, eponymous of the name *Rigveda*). In the eight books – Books 2 through 9 – that were composed the earliest, the hymns predominantly discuss [cosmology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmology "Cosmology"), rites required to earn the favour of the [gods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deity "Deity"),[^34] as well as praise them.[^35] [^36] The more recent books (Books 1 and 10) in part also deal with philosophical or speculative questions,[^36] virtues such as *[dāna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C4%81na "Dāna")* (charity) in society,[^37] questions about the origin of the universe and the nature of the divine,[^38] [^39] and other metaphysical issues in their hymns.[^40]

The hymns of the Rigveda are notably similar to the most archaic poems of the [Iranian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran "Iran") and [Greek language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language "Greek language") families, the *[Gathas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gatha_\(Zoroaster\) "Gatha (Zoroaster)")* of old [Avestan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avestan "Avestan") and *[Iliad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iliad "Iliad")* of [Homer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer "Homer").[^41] The Rigveda's preserved archaic syntax and morphology are of vital importance in the reconstruction of the common ancestor language [Proto-Indo-European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Indo-European "Proto-Indo-European").[^41] Some of its verses continue to be recited during Hindu prayer and celebration of [rites of passage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskara_\(rite_of_passage\) "Sanskara (rite of passage)") (such as [weddings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wedding "Wedding")), making it probably the world's oldest [religious text](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_text "Religious text") in continued use.[^42] [^43]

### Dating

According to Jamison and Brereton, in their 2014 translation of the *Rigveda*, the dating of this text "has been and is likely to remain a matter of contention and reconsideration". The dating proposals so far are all inferred from the style and the content within the hymns themselves.[^44] Philological estimates tend to date the bulk of the text to the second half of the second millennium BCE.[^1] Being composed in an early [Indo-Aryan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Indo-Aryan "Old Indo-Aryan") language, the hymns must post-date the [Indo-Iranian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Indo-Iranian "Proto-Indo-Iranian") separation, dated to roughly 2000 BCE.[^45] A reasonable date close to that of the composition of the core of the *Rigveda* is that of the [Mitanni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitanni "Mitanni") documents of northern Syria and Iraq (c. 1450 –1350 BCE), which also mention the Vedic gods such as Varuna, Mitra and Indra.[^46] [^47] Some scholars have suggested that the Rig Veda was composed on the banks of a river in Haraxvaiti province in southern [Afghanistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afghanistan "Afghanistan") ([Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_language "Persian language"): Harahvati; [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit "Sanskrit"): Sarasvati; possibly the [Helmand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helmand_Province "Helmand Province") or [Arghandab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arghandab_District,_Kandahar "Arghandab District, Kandahar")).[^48] [^49] Other evidence also points to a composition date close to 1400 BCE.[^50] [^51] The earliest texts were composed in the northwestern regions of the Indian subcontinent, and the more philosophical later texts were most likely composed in or around the region that is the modern era state of [Haryana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haryana "Haryana").[^52]

The *Rigveda* ' s core is accepted to date to the late [Bronze Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age "Bronze Age"), making it one of the few examples with an unbroken tradition. Its composition is usually dated to roughly between c. 1500 and 1000 BCE.[^1] According to [Michael Witzel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Witzel "Michael Witzel"), the codification of the *Rigveda* took place at the end of the Rigvedic period between c. 1200 and 1000 BCE, in the early [Kuru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuru_\(India\) "Kuru (India)") kingdom.[^31] [Asko Parpola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asko_Parpola "Asko Parpola") argues that the *Rigveda* was systematized around 1000 BCE, at the time of the Kuru kingdom.[^53]

No written records from such an early period survive, if any ever existed, but scholars are generally confident that the oral transmission of the texts is reliable: they are ceremonial literature, where the exact phonetic expression and its preservation were a part of the historic tradition.[^54] [^55] [^56]

The *Rigveda* is far more archaic than any other Indo-Aryan text. For this reason, it was in the centre of attention of [Western](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_world "Western world") scholarship from the times of [Max Müller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_M%C3%BCller "Max Müller") and [Rudolf Roth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolf_Roth "Rudolf Roth") onwards. The *Rigveda* records an early stage of [Vedic religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_Vedic_religion "Historical Vedic religion"). There are strong linguistic and cultural similarities with the [early Iranian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Iranian_languages "Early Iranian languages") [Avesta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avesta "Avesta"),[^57] [^58] deriving from the [Proto-Indo-Iranian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Indo-Iranian_language "Proto-Indo-Iranian language") times,[^59] often associated with the early [Andronovo culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andronovo_culture "Andronovo culture") of c. 2000 BCE.[^60]

The *Rigveda* offers no direct evidence of social or political systems in the Vedic era, whether ordinary or elite.[^61] Only hints such as [cattle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cattle "Cattle") raising and [horse racing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horse_racing "Horse racing") are discernible, and the text offers very general ideas about the ancient Indian society. There is no evidence, state Jamison and Brereton, of any elaborate, pervasive or structured [caste system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caste_system "Caste system").[^61] Social stratification seems embryonic, then and later a social ideal rather than a social reality.[^61] The society was semi-nomadic and pastoral with evidence of agriculture since hymns mention plow and celebrate agricultural divinities.[^62] There was division of labor and a complementary relationship between kings and poet-priests but no discussion of a relative status of social classes.[^61] Women in the *Rigveda* appear disproportionately as speakers in dialogue hymns, both as mythical or [divine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divinity "Divinity") [Indrani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indrani "Indrani"), [Apsaras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apsara "Apsara") [Urvasi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urvashi "Urvashi"), or [Yami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yamuna_in_Hinduism "Yamuna in Hinduism"), as well as Apāla Ātreyī (RV 8.91), Godhā (RV 10.134.6), Ghoṣā Kākṣīvatī (RV 10.39.40), Romaśā (RV 1.126.7), [Lopāmudrā](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lopamudra "Lopamudra") (RV 1.179.1–2), Viśvavārā Ātreyī (RV 5.28), Śacī Paulomī (RV 10.159), Śaśvatī Āṅgirasī (RV 8.1.34). The women of the *Rigveda* are quite outspoken and appear more sexually confident than men, in the text.[^61] Elaborate and aesthetic hymns on wedding suggest rites of passage had developed during the Rigvedic period.[^61] There is little evidence of [dowry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dowry "Dowry") and no evidence of [sati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sati_\(practice\) "Sati (practice)") in it or related Vedic texts.[^63]

The Rigvedic hymns mention [rice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asian_rice "Asian rice") and porridge, in hymns such as 8.83, 8.70, 8.77 and 1.61 in some versions of the text;[^64] however, there is no discussion of rice cultivation.[^62] The term *áyas* (metal) occurs in the *Rigveda*, but it is unclear which metal it was.[^65] Iron is not mentioned in *Rigveda*, something scholars have used to help date *Rigveda* to have been composed before 1000 BCE.[^52] Hymn 5.63 mentions "metal cloaked in gold", suggesting that metalworking had progressed in the Vedic culture.[^66]

Some of the names of [gods and goddesses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigvedic_deities "Rigvedic deities") found in the *Rigveda* are found amongst other belief systems based on [Proto-Indo-European religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Indo-European_religion "Proto-Indo-European religion"), while most of the words used share common [roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Indo-European_root "Proto-Indo-European root") with words from other [Indo-European languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-European_languages "Indo-European languages").[^67] However, about 300 words in the *Rigveda* are neither Indo-Aryan nor Indo-European, states the Sanskrit and Vedic literature scholar [Frits Staal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frits_Staal "Frits Staal").[^68] Of these 300, many – such as *kapardin*, *kumara*, *kumari*, *kikata*  – come from [Munda or proto-Munda languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munda_languages "Munda languages") found in the eastern and northeastern (Assamese) region of India, with roots in [Austroasiatic languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austroasiatic_languages "Austroasiatic languages"). The others in the list of 300 – such as *mleccha* and *nir*  – have Dravidian roots found in the southern region of India, or are of Tibeto-Burman origins. A few non-Indo-European words in the *Rigveda* – such as for camel, mustard and donkey – belong to a possibly lost Central Asian language.[^68] [^69] [^4] The linguistic sharing provides clear indications, states Michael Witzel, that the people who spoke Rigvedic Sanskrit already knew and interacted with Munda and Dravidian speakers.[^71]

## Text

### Composition

The "family books" (2–7) are associated with various clans and chieftains, containing hymns by members of the same clan in each book; but other clans are also represented in the *Rigveda*. The family books are associated with specific regions, and mention prominent [Bharata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bharatas_\(tribe\) "Bharatas (tribe)") and Pūru kings.[^72]

Tradition associates a [rishi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rishi "Rishi") (the composer) with each *ṛc* (verse) of the *Rigveda*. Most sūktas are attributed to single composers;[^5] for each of them the *Rigveda* includes a lineage-specific **āprī** hymn (a special sūkta of rigidly formulaic structure, used for rituals). In all, 10 families of rishis account for more than 95 per cent of the *ṛc* s.

The codification of the *Rigveda* took place late in the Rigvedic or rather in the early post-Rigvedic period at c. 1200 BCE, by members of the early [Kuru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuru_\(India\) "Kuru (India)") tribe, when the centre of Vedic culture moved east from the Punjab into what is now [Uttar Pradesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uttar_Pradesh "Uttar Pradesh").[^73] The *Rigveda* was codified by compiling the hymns, including the arrangement of the individual hymns in ten books, coeval with the composition of the younger Veda Samhitas.[^74] According to Witzel, the initial collection took place after the Bharata victory in the [Battle of the Ten Kings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Ten_Kings "Battle of the Ten Kings"), under king [Sudās](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sudas "Sudas"), over other Puru kings. This collection was an effort to reconcile various factions in the clans which were united in the Kuru kingdom under a Bharata king.[^75] [^6] This collection was re-arranged and expanded in the [Kuru Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuru_Kingdom "Kuru Kingdom"), reflecting the establishment of a new Bharata-Puru lineage and new srauta rituals.[^76] [^7]

The fixing of the [Vedic chant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedic_chant "Vedic chant") (by enforcing regular application of [sandhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandhi "Sandhi")) and of the padapatha (by dissolving Sandhi out of the earlier metrical text), occurred during the later Brahmana period, in roughly the 6th century BCE.[^78]

The surviving form of the *Rigveda* is based on an early [Iron Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Age_India "Iron Age India") collection that established the core 'family books' (mandalas [2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandala_2 "Mandala 2") – [7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandala_7 "Mandala 7"), ordered by author, deity and metre [^18]) and a later redaction, coeval with the redaction of the other [Vedas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Veda "Veda"), dating several centuries after the hymns were composed. This redaction also included some additions (contradicting the strict ordering scheme) and [orthoepic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthoepic "Orthoepic") changes to the [Vedic Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedic_Sanskrit "Vedic Sanskrit") such as the [regularization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regularization_\(linguistics\) "Regularization (linguistics)") of [sandhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandhi "Sandhi") (termed *orthoepische Diaskeuase* by Oldenberg, 1888).

### Organisation

#### Mandalas

The text is organized in ten "books", or *[maṇḍalas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandala "Mandala")* ("circles"), of varying age and length.[^79] The "family books", mandalas 2–7, are the oldest part of the *Rigveda* and the shortest books; they are arranged by length (decreasing length of hymns per book) and account for 38% of the text.[^80] [^81]

The hymns are arranged in collections each dealing with a particular deity: *Agni* comes first, *Indra* comes second, and so on. They are attributed and dedicated to a [rishi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rishi "Rishi") (sage) and his family of students.[^82] Within each collection, the hymns are arranged in descending order of the number of stanzas per hymn. If two hymns in the same collection have equal numbers of stanzas then they are arranged so that the number of syllables in the metre are in descending order.[^83] [^84] The second to seventh mandalas have a uniform format.[^80]

The [eighth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandala_8 "Mandala 8") and [ninth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandala_9 "Mandala 9") mandalas, comprising hymns of mixed age, account for 15% and 9%, respectively. The ninth mandala is entirely dedicated to [Soma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soma_\(drink\) "Soma (drink)") and the [Soma ritual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soma_ritual "Soma ritual"). The hymns in the ninth mandala are arranged by both their prosody structure ([chanda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chanda "Chanda")) and by their length.[^80]

The [first](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandala_1 "Mandala 1") and the [tenth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandala_10 "Mandala 10") mandalas are the youngest; they are also the longest books, of 191 suktas each, accounting for 37% of the text. Nevertheless, some of the hymns in mandalas 8, 1 and 10 may still belong to an earlier period and may be as old as the material in the family books.[^85] The first mandala has a unique arrangement not found in the other nine mandalas. The first 84 hymns of the tenth mandala have a structure different from the remaining hymns in it.[^80]

Each mandala consists of hymns or **sūkta** s (**[su-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Su- "Su-") + [ukta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C4%81c "Vāc")**, literally, "well recited, [eulogy](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/eulogy "wikt:eulogy") ") intended for various [rituals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yajna "Yajna"). The *sūkta* s in turn consist of individual stanzas called **ṛc** ("praise", *pl.***ṛcas**), which are further analysed into units of verse called **[pada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pada_\(foot\) "Pada (foot)")** (" [foot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foot_\(poetry\) "Foot (poetry)") " or step).

The hymns of the *Rigveda* are in different poetic metres in Vedic Sanskrit. The [metres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit_prosody "Sanskrit prosody") most used in the *ṛcas* are the [gayatri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gayatri "Gayatri") (3 verses of 8 syllables), [anushtubh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anushtubh "Anushtubh") (4×8), [trishtubh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trishtubh "Trishtubh") (4×11) and [jagati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedic_metre "Vedic metre") (4×12). The trishtubh metre (40%) and gayatri metre (25%) dominate in the *Rigveda*.[^86] [^87] [^88]

### Transmission

As with the other Vedas, the redacted text has been handed down in several versions, including the *[Padapatha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Padapatha "Padapatha")*, in which each word is isolated in [pausa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pausa "Pausa") form and is used for just one way of memorization; and the *[Samhitapatha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samhitapatha "Samhitapatha")*, which combines words according to the rules of sandhi (the process being described in the *[Pratisakhya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratisakhya "Pratisakhya")*) and is the memorized text used for recitation.

The *Padapatha* and the *Pratisakhya* anchor the text's true meaning,[^90] and the fixed text was preserved with unparalleled fidelity for more than a millennium by [oral tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oral_tradition "Oral tradition") alone.[^46] In order to achieve this the oral tradition prescribed very structured enunciation, involving breaking down the Sanskrit [compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compound_word "Compound word") into [stems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_stem "Word stem") and inflections, as well as certain permutations. This interplay with sounds gave rise to a [scholarly tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vy%C4%81kara%E1%B9%87a "Vyākaraṇa") of [morphology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morphology_\(linguistics\) "Morphology (linguistics)") and [phonetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phonetics "Phonetics").

It is unclear as to when the *Rigveda* was first written down. The oldest surviving manuscripts have been discovered in [Nepal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nepal "Nepal") and date to c. 1040 CE.[^16] [^91] According to Witzel, the Paippalada Samhita tradition points to written manuscripts c. 800 –1000 CE.[^92] The Upanishads were likely in the written form earlier, about mid-1st millennium CE ([Gupta Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gupta_Empire "Gupta Empire") period).[^46] [^93] Attempts to write the Vedas may have been made "towards the end of the 1st millennium BCE". The early attempts may have been unsuccessful given the *Smriti* rules that forbade the writing down the Vedas, states Witzel.[^46] The oral tradition continued as a means of transmission until modern times.[^94]

### Recensions

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/51/Late_Vedic_Culture_%281100-500_BCE%29.png/960px-Late_Vedic_Culture_%281100-500_BCE%29.png)

Geographical distribution of the Late Vedic Period. Each major region had its own recension of Rig Veda ( Śākhās ), and the versions varied. 16

Several [shakhas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakha "Shakha") (from skt. *śākhā* f. "branch", i. e. "recension") of the Rig Veda are known to have existed in the past. Of these, [Śākala Śākhā](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakala_Shakha "Shakala Shakha") (named after the scholar [Śākalya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakalya "Shakalya")) is the only one to have survived in its entirety. Another śākhā that may have survived is the Bāṣkala, although this is uncertain.[^95] [^96] [^97]

The surviving padapāṭha version of the *Rigveda* text is ascribed to Śākalya.[^98] The *[Śākala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakala_Shakha "Shakala Shakha")* recension has 1,017 regular hymns, and an appendix of 11 **[vālakhilya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valakhilya "Valakhilya")** hymns [^99] which are now customarily included in the 8th mandala (as 8.49–8.59), for a total of 1028 hymns.[^100] The *Bāṣkala* recension includes eight of these *vālakhilya* hymns among its regular hymns, making a total of 1025 regular hymns for this śākhā.[^101] In addition, the *Bāṣkala* recension has its own appendix of 98 hymns, the [Khilani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khilani "Khilani").[^102]

In the 1877 edition of Aufrecht, the 1028 hymns of the *Rigveda* contain a total of 10,552 *ṛc* s, or 39,831 padas. The [Shatapatha Brahmana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shatapatha_Brahmana "Shatapatha Brahmana") gives the number of syllables to be 432,000,[^103] while the metrical text of van Nooten and Holland (1994) has a total of 395,563 syllables (or an average of 9.93 syllables per pada); counting the number of syllables is not straightforward because of issues with sandhi and the post-Rigvedic pronunciation of syllables like súvar as svàr.

Three other shakhas are mentioned in *Caraṇavyuha*, a [pariśiṣṭa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pari%C5%9Bi%E1%B9%A3%E1%B9%ADa "Pariśiṣṭa") (supplement) of Yajurveda: Māṇḍukāyana, Aśvalāyana and [Śaṅkhāyana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sankhyayana "Sankhyayana"). The Atharvaveda lists two more shakhas. The differences between all these shakhas are very minor, limited to varying order of content and inclusion (or non-inclusion) of a few verses. The following information is known about the shakhas other than [Śākala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%9A%C4%81kala_Sh%C4%81kha "Śākala Shākha") and Bāṣkala:[^104]

- Māṇḍukāyana: Perhaps the oldest of the Rigvedic shakhas.
- Aśvalāyana: Includes 212 verses, all of which are newer than the other Rigvedic hymns.
- [Śaṅkhāyana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sankhyayana "Sankhyayana"): Very similar to Aśvalāyana
- Saisiriya: Mentioned in the *Rigveda* [Pratisakhya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratisakhya "Pratisakhya"). Very similar to Śākala, with a few additional verses; might have derived from or merged with it.

### Manuscripts

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/71/1500-1200_BCE_Rigveda%2C_manuscript_page_sample_i%2C_Mandala_1%2C_Hymn_1_%28Sukta_1%29%2C_Adhyaya_1%2C_lines_1.1.1_to_1.1.9%2C_Sanskrit%2C_Devanagari.jpg/960px-1500-1200_BCE_Rigveda%2C_manuscript_page_sample_i%2C_Mandala_1%2C_Hymn_1_%28Sukta_1%29%2C_Adhyaya_1%2C_lines_1.1.1_to_1.1.9%2C_Sanskrit%2C_Devanagari.jpg)

Rigveda manuscript page, Mandala 1, Hymn 1 (Sukta 1), lines 1.1.1 to 1.1.9 (Sanskrit, Devanagari script)

The *Rigveda* hymns were composed and preserved by [oral tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oral_tradition "Oral tradition"). They were memorized and verbally transmitted with "unparalleled fidelity" across generations for many centuries.[^46] [^105] According to Barbara West, it was probably first written down about the 3rd-century BCE.[^106] [^107] The manuscripts were made from [birch bark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birch_bark_manuscript "Birch bark manuscript") or [palm leaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palm_leaf_manuscript "Palm leaf manuscript"), which decompose and therefore were routinely copied over the generations to help preserve the text.

#### Versions

There are, for example, thirty manuscripts of *Rigveda* at the [Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhandarkar_Oriental_Research_Institute "Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute"), collected in the 19th century by [Georg Bühler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_B%C3%BChler "Georg Bühler"), Franz Kielhorn and others, originating from different parts of India, including [Kashmir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmir "Kashmir"), [Gujarat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gujarat "Gujarat"), the then [Rajaputana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rajaputana "Rajaputana"), and [Central Provinces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Provinces "Central Provinces"). They were transferred to [Deccan College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deccan_College_\(Pune\) "Deccan College (Pune)"), [Pune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pune "Pune"), in the late 19th century. They are in the [Sharada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharada_script "Sharada script") and [Devanagari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devanagari "Devanagari") scripts, written on birch bark and paper. The oldest of the Pune collection is dated to 1464 CE. These thirty manuscripts were added to [UNESCO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UNESCO "UNESCO") 's [Memory of the World International Register](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memory_of_the_World_Register "Memory of the World Register") in 2007.[^108] [^109]

Of these thirty manuscripts, nine contain the samhita text, five have the [padapatha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Padapatha "Padapatha") in addition. Thirteen contain Sayana's commentary. At least five manuscripts (MS. no. 1/A1879-80, 1/A1881-82, 331/1883-84 and 5/Viś I) have preserved the complete text of the *Rigveda*. MS no. 5/1875-76, written on birch bark in bold Sharada, was only in part used by [Max Müller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_M%C3%BCller "Max Müller") for his edition of the *Rigveda* with Sayana's commentary.

Müller used 24 manuscripts then available to him in Europe, while the Pune Edition used over five dozen manuscripts, but the editors of Pune Edition could not procure many manuscripts used by Müller and by the Bombay Edition, as well as from some other sources; hence the total number of extant manuscripts known then must surpass perhaps eighty at least.[^110]

#### Scripts

*Rigveda* manuscripts in paper, palm leaves and birch bark form, either in full or in portions, have been discovered in the following Indic scripts:

- [Devanagari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devanagari "Devanagari") (Rajasthan, Maharashtra, Uttar Pradesh, Nepal) [^111] [^112] [^113]
- [Grantha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grantha_script "Grantha script") (Tamil Nadu) [^114] [^115]
- [Malayalam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malayalam_script "Malayalam script") (Kerala) [^116]
- [Nandinagari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nandinagari "Nandinagari") (South India) [^117]
- [Sharada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharada_script "Sharada script") (Kashmir) [^118] [^119]

#### Comparison

The various *Rigveda* manuscripts discovered so far show some differences. Broadly, the most studied Śākala recension has 1017 hymns, includes an appendix of eleven *valakhīlya* hymns which are often counted with the eighth mandala, for a total of 1028 metrical hymns. The Bāṣakala version of *Rigveda* includes eight of these *vālakhilya* hymns among its regular hymns, making a total of 1025 hymns in the main text for this śākhā. The Bāṣakala text also has an appendix of 98 hymns, called the *Khilani*, bringing the total to 1,123 hymns. The manuscripts of Śākala recension of the *Rigveda* have about 10,600 verses, organized into ten Books (*Mandalas*).[^120] [^121] Books 2 through 7 are internally homogeneous in style, while Books 1, 8 and 10 are compilation of verses of internally different styles suggesting that these books are likely a collection of compositions by many authors.[^121]

The first mandala is the largest, with 191 hymns and 2006 verses, and it was added to the text after Books 2 through 9. The last, or the 10th Book, also has 191 hymns but 1754 verses, making it the second largest. The language analytics suggest the 10th Book, chronologically, was composed and added last.[^121] The content of the 10th Book also suggest that the authors knew and relied on the contents of the first nine books.[^121]

The *Rigveda* is the largest of the four Vedas, and many of its verses appear in the other Vedas.[^122] Almost all of the 1875 verses found in [Samaveda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samaveda "Samaveda") are taken from different parts of the *Rigveda*, either once or as repetition, and rewritten in a chant song form. Books 8 and 9 of the *Rigveda* are by far the largest source of verses for Sama Veda. Book 10 contributes the largest number of the 1350 verses of *Rigveda* found in [Atharvaveda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atharvaveda "Atharvaveda"), or about one fifth of the 5987 verses in the Atharvaveda text.[^121] A bulk of 1875 ritual-focussed verses of [Yajurveda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yajurveda "Yajurveda"), in its numerous versions, also borrow and build upon the foundation of verses in *Rigveda*.[^122] [^123]

## Contents

Altogether the Rigveda consists of:

- the *[Samhita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samhita "Samhita")* (hymns to the deities, the oldest part of the *Rigveda*)
- the *[Brahmana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmana "Brahmana")* s, commentaries on the hymns
- the *[Aranyaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aranyaka "Aranyaka")* s or "forest books"
- the *[Upanishad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upanishads "Upanishads")* s

In western usage, "Rigveda" usually refers to the *Rigveda* Samhita, while the Brahmanas are referred to as the "Rigveda Brahmanas" (etc.). Technically speaking, however, "the Rigveda" refers to the entire body of texts transmitted along with the Samhita portion. Different bodies of commentary were transmitted in the different [shakhas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakha "Shakha") or "schools". Only a small portion of these texts has been preserved: The texts of only two out of five shakhas mentioned by the [Rigveda Pratishakhya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratishakhyas "Pratishakhyas") have survived. The late (15th or 16th century) *[Shri Guru Charitra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shri_Guru_Charitra "Shri Guru Charitra")* even claims the existence of twelve Rigvedic shakhas. The two surviving Rigvedic corpora are those of the *Śākala* and the *Bāṣkala* shakhas.

### Hymns

The Rigvedic hymns are dedicated to various deities, chief of whom are [Indra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indra "Indra"), a heroic god praised for having slain his enemy [Vrtra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vrtra "Vrtra"); [Agni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agni "Agni"), the sacrificial fire; and [Soma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soma_\(drink\) "Soma (drink)"), the sacred potion or the plant it is made from. Equally prominent gods are the [Adityas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adityas "Adityas") or Asura gods [Mitra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitra_\(Vedic\) "Mitra (Vedic)") – [Varuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varuna "Varuna") and [Ushas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ushas "Ushas") (the dawn). Also invoked are [Savitr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savitr "Savitr"), [Vishnu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vishnu "Vishnu"), [Rudra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudra "Rudra"), [Pushan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pushan "Pushan"), [Brihaspati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brihaspati "Brihaspati") or [Brahmanaspati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmanaspati "Brahmanaspati"), as well as deified natural phenomena such as [Dyaus Pita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dyaus_Pita "Dyaus Pita") (the shining sky, Father Heaven), [Prithivi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prithivi "Prithivi") (the earth, Mother Earth), [Surya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surya "Surya") (the sun god), [Vayu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vayu "Vayu") or Vata (the wind), [Apas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ap_\(water\) "Ap (water)") (the waters), [Parjanya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parjanya "Parjanya") (the thunder and rain), [Vac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C4%81c "Vāc") (the word), many [rivers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigvedic_rivers "Rigvedic rivers") (notably the [Sapta Sindhu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sapta_Sindhu "Sapta Sindhu"), and the [Sarasvati River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarasvati_River "Sarasvati River")). The [Adityas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adityas "Adityas"), Vasus, Rudras, Sadhyas, [Ashvins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashvins "Ashvins"), [Maruts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maruts "Maruts"), [Rbhus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rbhus "Rbhus"), and the [Vishvadevas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vishvadevas "Vishvadevas") ("all-gods") as well as the "thirty-three gods" are the groups of deities mentioned.

- [Mandala 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandala_1 "Mandala 1") comprises 191 hymns. Hymn 1.1 is addressed to [Agni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agni "Agni"), and his name is the first word of the *Rigveda*. The remaining hymns are mainly addressed to Agni and [Indra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indra "Indra"), as well as Varuna, Mitra, the Ashvins, the Maruts, Usas, Surya, Rbhus, Rudra, Vayu, Brhaspati, Visnu, Heaven and Earth, and all the Gods. This Mandala is dated to have been added to the *Rigveda* after Mandala 2 through 9, and includes the philosophical Riddle Hymn 1.164, which inspires chapters in later Upanishads such as the [Mundaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mundaka_Upanishad "Mundaka Upanishad").[^36] [^124] [^125]
- [Mandala 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandala_2 "Mandala 2") comprises 43 hymns, mainly to [Agni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agni "Agni") and [Indra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indra "Indra"). It is chiefly attributed to the Rishi **gṛtsamada śaunahotra**.
- [Mandala 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandala_3 "Mandala 3") comprises 62 hymns, mainly to [Agni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agni "Agni") and [Indra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indra "Indra") and the Vishvedevas. The verse 3.62.10 has great importance in [Hinduism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism "Hinduism") as the [Gayatri Mantra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gayatri_Mantra "Gayatri Mantra"). Most hymns in this book are attributed to **viśvāmitra gāthinaḥ**.
- [Mandala 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandala_4 "Mandala 4") comprises 58 hymns, mainly to [Agni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agni "Agni") and [Indra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indra "Indra") as well as the Rbhus, Ashvins, Brhaspati, Vayu, Usas, etc. Most hymns in this book are attributed to **vāmadeva gautama**.
- [Mandala 5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandala_5 "Mandala 5") comprises 87 hymns, mainly to [Agni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agni "Agni") and [Indra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indra "Indra"), the [Visvedevas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visvedevas "Visvedevas") ("all the gods'), the [Maruts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maruts "Maruts"), the twin-deity [Mitra-Varuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitra-Varuna "Mitra-Varuna") and the [Asvins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asvins "Asvins"). Two hymns each are dedicated to [Ushas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ushas "Ushas") (the dawn) and to [Savitr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savitr "Savitr"). Most hymns in this book are attributed to the **atri** clan.
- [Mandala 6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandala_6 "Mandala 6") comprises 75 hymns, mainly to [Agni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agni "Agni") and [Indra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indra "Indra"), all the gods, Pusan, Ashvin, Usas, etc. Most hymns in this book are attributed to the **bārhaspatya** family of [Angirasas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angiras_\(sage\) "Angiras (sage)").
- [Mandala 7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandala_7 "Mandala 7") comprises 104 hymns, to [Agni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agni "Agni"), [Indra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indra "Indra"), the [Visvadevas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visvadevas "Visvadevas"), the [Maruts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maruts "Maruts"), [Mitra-Varuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitra-Varuna "Mitra-Varuna"), the [Asvins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asvins "Asvins"), [Ushas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ushas "Ushas"), [Indra-Varuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indra-Varuna "Indra-Varuna"), [Varuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varuna "Varuna"), [Vayu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vayu "Vayu") (the wind), two each to [Sarasvati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarasvati "Sarasvati") (ancient river/goddess of learning) and [Vishnu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vishnu "Vishnu"), and to others. Most hymns in this book are attributed to **vasiṣṭha maitravaruṇi**.
- [Mandala 8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandala_8 "Mandala 8") comprises 103 hymns to various gods. Hymns 8.49 to 8.59 are the apocryphal **vālakhilya**. Hymns 1–48 and 60–66 are attributed to the **kāṇva** clan, the rest to other (Angirasa) poets.
- [Mandala 9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandala_9 "Mandala 9") comprises 114 hymns, entirely devoted to *[Soma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soma_\(drink\) "Soma (drink)") Pavamana*, the cleansing of the sacred potion of the Vedic religion.
- [Mandala 10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandala_10 "Mandala 10") comprises additional 191 hymns, frequently in later language, addressed to [Agni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agni "Agni"), [Indra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indra "Indra") and various other deities. It contains the [Nadistuti sukta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nadistuti_sukta "Nadistuti sukta") which is in praise of rivers and is important for the reconstruction of the geography of the Vedic civilization and the [Purusha sukta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purusha_sukta "Purusha sukta") which has been important in studies of Vedic sociology.[^61] It also contains the [Nasadiya sukta (Hymn of Creation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasadiya_sukta "Nasadiya sukta") (10.129) which deals with multiple speculations about the creation of universe, and whether anyone can know the right answer.[^38] The marriage hymns (10.85) and the death hymns (10.10–18) still are of great importance in the performance of the corresponding [Grhya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grhya "Grhya") rituals.

### Rigveda Brahmanas

Of the Brahmanas that were handed down in the schools of the **Bahvṛcas** (i.e. "possessed of many verses"), as the followers of the *Rigveda* are called, two have come down to us, namely those of the Aitareyins and the Kaushitakins. The *[Aitareya-brahmana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aitareya-brahmana "Aitareya-brahmana")* [^126] and the [*Kaushitaki-* (or *Sankhayana-*) *brahmana*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sankhyayana_Brahmana "Sankhyayana Brahmana") evidently have for their groundwork the same stock of traditional exegetic matter. They differ, however, considerably as regards both the arrangement of this matter and their stylistic handling of it, with the exception of the numerous legends common to both, in which the discrepancy is comparatively slight. There is also a certain amount of material peculiar to each of them.

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/03/1500-1200_BCE%2C_Devi_sukta%2C_Rigveda_10.125.1-2%2C_Sanskrit%2C_Devanagari%2C_manuscript_page_1735_CE_%281792_VS%29.jpg/500px-1500-1200_BCE%2C_Devi_sukta%2C_Rigveda_10.125.1-2%2C_Sanskrit%2C_Devanagari%2C_manuscript_page_1735_CE_%281792_VS%29.jpg)

Devi sukta, which highlights the goddess tradition of Hinduism is found in Rigveda hymns 10.125. It is cited in Devi Mahatmya and is recited every year during the Durga Puja festival.

The Kaushitaka is, upon the whole, far more concise in its style and more systematic in its arrangement features which would lead one to infer that it is probably the more modern work of the two. It consists of 30 chapters (*adhyaya*); while the Aitareya has 40, divided into eight books (or pentads, *pancaka*), of five chapters each. The last 10 adhyayas of the latter work are, however, clearly a later addition though they must have already formed part of it at the time of [Pāṇini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C4%81%E1%B9%87ini "Pāṇini") (c. 5th century BCE), if, as seems probable, one of his grammatical sutras, regulating the formation of the names of Brahmanas, consisting of 30 and 40 adhyayas, refers to these two works. In this last portion occurs the well-known legend (also found in the Shankhayana-sutra, but not in the Kaushitaki-brahmana) of [Shunahshepa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shunahshepa "Shunahshepa"), whom his father Ajigarta sells and offers to slay, the recital of which formed part of the inauguration of kings.

While the Aitareya deals almost exclusively with the Soma sacrifice, the Kaushitaka, in its first six chapters, treats of the several kinds of *haviryajna*, or offerings of rice, milk, ghee, etc., whereupon follows the Soma sacrifice in this way, that chapters 7–10 contain the practical ceremonial and 11–30 the recitations (*shastra*) of the hotar. Sayana, in the introduction to his commentary on the work, ascribes the Aitareya to the sage Mahidasa Aitareya (i.e. son of Itara), also mentioned elsewhere as a philosopher; and it seems likely enough that this person arranged the Brahmana and founded the school of the Aitareyins. Regarding the authorship of the sister work we have no information, except that the opinion of the sage Kaushitaki is frequently referred to in it as authoritative, and generally in opposition to the Paingya—the Brahmana, it would seem, of a rival school, the Paingins. Probably, therefore, it is just what one of the manuscripts calls it—the Brahmana of Sankhayana (composed) in accordance with the views of Kaushitaki.

Each of these two Brahmanas is supplemented by a "forest book", or [Aranyaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aranyaka "Aranyaka"). The *Aitareyaranyaka* is not a uniform production. It consists of five books (*aranyaka*), three of which, the first and the last two, are of a liturgical nature, treating of the ceremony called *mahavrata*, or great vow. The last of these books, composed in sutra form, is, however, doubtless of later origin, and is, indeed, ascribed by Hindu authorities either to Shaunaka or to Ashvalayana. The second and third books, on the other hand, are purely speculative, and are also styled the *Bahvrca-brahmana-upanishad*. Again, the last four chapters of the second book are usually singled out as the [Aitareya Upanishad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aitareya_Upanishad "Aitareya Upanishad"),[^127] ascribed, like its Brahmana (and the first book), to Mahidasa Aitareya; and the third book is also referred to as the *Samhita-upanishad*. As regards the *Kaushitaki-aranyaka*, this work consists of 15 adhyayas, the first two (treating of the mahavrata ceremony) and the 7th and 8th of which correspond to the first, fifth, and third books of the Aitareyaranyaka, respectively, whilst the four adhyayas usually inserted between them constitute the highly interesting [Kaushitaki (Brahmana-) Upanishad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaushitaki_Upanishad "Kaushitaki Upanishad"),[^128] of which we possess two different recensions. The remaining portions (9–15) of the Aranyaka treat of the vital airs, the internal Agnihotra, etc., ending with the *vamsha*, or succession of teachers.

#### Significance

The text is a highly stylized poetical Vedic Sanskrit with praise addressed to the Vedic gods and chieftains. Most hymns, according to Witzel, were intended to be recited at the annual New Year Soma ritual.[^129] The text also includes some nonritual poetry,[^129] fragments of mythology, archaic formulas, and a number of hymns with early philosophical speculations.[^130] Composed by the poets of different clans, including famed Vedic *rishis* (sages) such as [Vishvamitra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vishvamitra "Vishvamitra") and [Vasishtha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vasishtha "Vasishtha"), these signify the power of prestige therewith to *vac* (speech, sound), a tradition set in place.[^129] The text introduced the prized concepts such as *Rta* (active realization of truth, cosmic harmony) which inspired the later Hindu concept of [Dharma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharma "Dharma"). The Rigvedic verses formulate this *Rta* as effected by *[Brahman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahman "Brahman")*, a significant and non-self-evident truth.[^129] The text also contains hymns of "highly poetical value" – some in dialogue form, along with love stories that likely inspired later Epic and classical poets of Hinduism, states Witzel.[^130]

According to Nadkarni, several hymns of the *Rigveda* embed cherished virtues and ethical statements. For example, verses 5.82.7, 6.44.8, 9.113.4, 10.133.6 and 10.190.1 mention truthful speech, truthful action, self-discipline and righteousness.[^131] [^132] Hymn 10.117 presents the significance of charity and of generosity between human beings, how helping someone in need is ultimately in the self-interest of the helper, its importance to an individual and the society.[^37] [^133] According to Jamison and Brereton, hymns 9.112 and 9.113 poetically state, "what everyone \[humans and all living beings\] really want is gain or an easy life", even a water drop has a goal – namely, "simply to seek Indra". These hymns present the imagery of being in heaven as "freedom, joy and satisfaction", a theme that appears in the Hindu Upanishads to characterize their teachings of self-realization.[^134]

#### Monism debate

While the older hymns of the *Rigveda* reflect [sacrificial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacrifice "Sacrifice") ritual typical of [polytheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytheism "Polytheism"),[^135] its younger parts, specifically mandalas 1 and 10, have been noted as containing [monistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monism "Monism") or [henotheistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henotheism "Henotheism") speculations.[^135]

> [Nasadiya Sukta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasadiya_Sukta "Nasadiya Sukta") ([10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandala_10 "Mandala 10").129):
> 
> There was neither non-existence nor existence then;  
> Neither the realm of space, nor the sky which is beyond;  
> What stirred? Where? In whose protection?
> 
> There was neither death nor immortality then;  
> No distinguishing sign of night nor of day;  
> That One breathed, windless, by its own impulse;  
> Other than that there was nothing beyond.
> 
> Darkness there was at first, by darkness hidden;  
> Without distinctive marks, this all was water;  
> That which, becoming, by the void was covered;  
> That One by force of heat came into being;
> 
> Who really knows? Who will here proclaim it?  
> Whence was it produced? Whence is this creation?  
> Gods came afterwards, with the creation of this universe.  
> Who then knows whence it has arisen?
> 
> Whether God's will created it, or whether He was mute;  
> Perhaps it formed itself, or perhaps it did not;  
> Only He who is its overseer in highest heaven knows,  
> Only He knows, or perhaps He does not know.

— *Rigveda* 10.129 (Abridged, Tr: Kramer / Christian) [^38] This hymn is one of the roots of [Hindu philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_philosophy "Hindu philosophy").[^136]

A widely cited example of such speculations is hymn 1.164.46:

> They call him Indra, Mitra, Varuna, Agni, and he is heavenly nobly-winged Garutman.  
> To what is One, sages give many a title they call it Agni, Yama, Matarisvan.

— Rigveda 1.164.46, Translated by Ralph Griffith [^137] [^138]

[Max Müller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_M%C3%BCller "Max Müller") notably introduced the term " [henotheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henotheism "Henotheism") " for the philosophy expressed here, avoiding the connotations of "monotheism" in Judeo-Christian tradition.[^138] [^139] Other widely cited examples of [monistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monism "Monism") tendencies include hymns 1.164, 8.36 and 10.31,[^140] [^141] Other scholars state that the *Rigveda* includes an emerging diversity of thought, including monotheism, polytheism, henotheism and pantheism, the choice left to the preference of the worshipper.[^142] and the [Nasadiya Sukta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasadiya_Sukta "Nasadiya Sukta") (10.129), one of the most widely cited Rigvedic hymns in popular western presentations.

Ruse (2015) commented on the old discussion of "monotheism" vs. "henotheism" vs. "monism" by noting an " [atheistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheism "Atheism") streak" in hymns such as [10.130](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Rig_Veda/Mandala_10/Hymn_130 "wikisource:The Rig Veda/Mandala 10/Hymn 130").[^143]

Examples from [Mandala 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandala_1 "Mandala 1") adduced to illustrate the "metaphysical" nature of the contents of the younger hymns include: 1.164.34: "What is the ultimate limit of the earth?", "What is the center of the universe?", "What is the semen of the cosmic horse?", "What is the ultimate source of human speech?"; 1.164.34: "Who gave blood, soul, spirit to the earth?", "How could the unstructured universe give origin to this structured world?"; 1.164.5: "Where does the sun hide in the night?", "Where do gods live?"; 1.164.6: "What, where is the unborn support for the born universe?";[1.164](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Rig_Veda/Mandala_1/Hymn_164 "s:The Rig Veda/Mandala 1/Hymn 164").20 (a hymn that is widely cited in the Upanishads as the parable of the Body and the Soul): "Two birds with fair wings, inseparable companions; Have found refuge in the same sheltering tree. One incessantly eats from the fig tree; the other, not eating, just looks on.".[^40]

### Shruti

The Vedas as a whole are classed as " [shruti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shruti "Shruti") " in Hindu tradition. This has been compared to the concept of [divine revelation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine_revelation "Divine revelation") in Western religious tradition, but Staal argues that "it is nowhere stated that the Veda was revealed", and that *shruti* simply means "that what is heard, in the sense that it is transmitted from father to son or from teacher to pupil".[^144] The *Rigveda*, or other Vedas, do not anywhere assert that they are [apauruṣeyā](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apauru%E1%B9%A3ey%C4%81 "Apauruṣeyā"), and this reverential term appears only centuries after the end of the Vedic period in the texts of the [Mimamsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mimamsa "Mimamsa") school of Hindu philosophy.[^144] [^145] [^146] The text of the *Rigveda* suggests it was "composed by poets, human individuals whose names were household words" in the Vedic age, states Staal.[^144]

The authors of the [*Brāhmana*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmanas "Brahmanas") literature discussed and interpreted the Vedic ritual.

### Sanskrit grammarians

[Yaska](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yaska "Yaska") (4th c. BCE), a [lexicographer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lexicography "Lexicography"), was an early commentator of the *Rigveda* by discussing the meanings of difficult words. In his book titled *[Nirukta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nirukta "Nirukta")* Yaska asserts that the *Rigveda* in the ancient tradition can be interpreted in three ways – from the perspective of religious rites (*adhiyajna*), from the perspective of the deities (*adhidevata*), and from the perspective of the soul (*adhyatman*).[^147] The fourth way to interpret the *Rigveda* also emerged in the ancient times, wherein the gods mentioned were viewed as symbolism for legendary individuals or narratives.[^147] It was generally accepted that creative poets often embed and express double meanings, ellipses and novel ideas to inspire the reader.[^147]

By the period of [Puranic Hinduism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puranic_Hinduism "Puranic Hinduism"), in the medieval period, the language of the hymns had become "almost entirely unintelligible", and their interpretation mostly hinged on [mystical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mysticism "Mysticism") ideas and [sound symbolism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_symbolism "Sound symbolism").[^148] [^149] [^150]

According to the Puranic tradition, Ved Vyasa compiled all the four Vedas, along with the Mahabharata and the Puranas. Vyasa then taught the *Rigveda* samhita to Paila, who started the oral tradition.[^151] An alternate version states that Shakala compiled the *Rigveda* from the teachings of Vedic rishis, and one of the manuscript recensions mentions Shakala.[^151]

[Madhvacharya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madhvacharya "Madhvacharya"), a Hindu philosopher of the 13th century, provided a commentary of the first 40 hymns of the *Rigveda* in his book *Rig Bhashyam*.[^9] In the 14th century, [*Sāyana*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayana "Sayana") wrote an exhaustive commentary on the complete text of the *Rigveda* in his book *Rigveda Samhita*.[^10] This book was translated from Sanskrit to English by [Max Müller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_M%C3%BCller "Max Müller") in the year 1856. [H.H. Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H.H._Wilson "H.H. Wilson") also translated this book into English as *Rigveda Sanhita* in the year 1856. Sayanacharya studied at the [Sringeri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sringeri_Sharada_Peetham "Sringeri Sharada Peetham") monastery.

A number of other commentaries (**bhāṣya* s*) were written during the medieval period, including the commentaries by Skandasvamin (pre-Sayana, roughly of the [Gupta period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gupta_period "Gupta period")), [Udgitha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Udgatr "Udgatr") (pre-Sayana), Venkata-Madhava (pre-Sayana, c. 10th to 12th centuries) and [Mudgala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mudgala_Purana "Mudgala Purana") (after Sayana, an abbreviated version of Sayana's commentary).[^152]

In the 19th and early 20th centuries, reformers like [Swami Dayananda Saraswati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swami_Dayananda_Saraswati "Swami Dayananda Saraswati") (founder of the [Arya Samaj](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arya_Samaj "Arya Samaj")) and [Sri Aurobindo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sri_Aurobindo "Sri Aurobindo") (founder of [Sri Aurobindo Ashram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sri_Aurobindo_Ashram "Sri Aurobindo Ashram")) discussed the philosophies of the Vedas. According to Robson, Dayananda believed "there were no errors in the Vedas (including the *Rigveda*), and if anyone showed him an error, he would maintain that it was a corruption added later".[^153]

According to Dayananda and Aurobindo the Vedic scholars had a monotheistic conception.[^154] [Sri Aurobindo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sri_Aurobindo "Sri Aurobindo") gave commentaries, general interpretation guidelines, and a partial translation in *The secret of Veda* (1946).[^11] Sri Aurobindo finds Sayana's interpretation to be ritualistic in nature, and too often having inconsistent interpretations of Vedic terms, trying to fit the meaning to a narrow mold. According to Aurobindo, if Sayana's interpretation were to be accepted, it would seem as if the Rig Veda belongs to an unquestioning tradition of faith, starting from an original error.[^155] Aurobindo attempted to interpret hymns to Agni in the *Rigveda* as mystical.[^154] Aurobindo states that the Vedic hymns were a quest after a higher truth, define the *Rta* (basis of [Dharma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharma "Dharma")), conceive life in terms of a struggle between the forces of light and darkness, and sought the ultimate reality.[^154]

### Contemporary Hinduism

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/37/1500-1200_BCE%2C_Vivaha_sukta%2C_Rigveda_10.85.16-27%2C_Sanskrit%2C_Devanagari%2C_manuscript_page.jpg/500px-1500-1200_BCE%2C_Vivaha_sukta%2C_Rigveda_10.85.16-27%2C_Sanskrit%2C_Devanagari%2C_manuscript_page.jpg)

The hymn 10.85 of the Rigveda includes the Vivaha-sukta (above). Its recitation continues to be a part of Hindu wedding rituals. 156 157

The *Rigveda*, in contemporary Hinduism, has been a reminder of the ancient cultural heritage and point of pride for Hindus, with some hymns still in use in major [rites of passage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskara_\(rite_of_passage\) "Sanskara (rite of passage)") ceremonies, but the literal acceptance of most of the textual essence is long gone.[^158] [^159] Musicians and dance groups celebrate the text as a mark of Hindu heritage, through incorporating Rigvedic hymns in their compositions, such as in *Hamsadhvani* and *Subhapantuvarali* of [Carnatic music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnatic_music "Carnatic music"), and these have remained popular among the [Hindus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu "Hindu") for decades.[^158]

According to Axel Michaels, "most Indians today pay lip service to the Veda and have no regard for the contents of the text." [^160] According to [Louis Renou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Renou "Louis Renou"), the Vedic texts are a distant object, and "even in the most orthodox domains, the reverence to the Vedas has come to be a simple raising of the hat".[^158] According to Andrea Pinkney, "the social history and context of the Vedic texts are extremely distant from contemporary Hindu religious beliefs and practice", and the reverence for the Vedas in contemporary Hinduism illustrates the respect among the Hindus for their heritage.[^158]

#### Hindu nationalism

The Rig Veda plays a role in the modern construction of a Hindu identity, portraying Hindus as the original inhabitants of India. The *Rigveda* has been referred to in the " [Indigenous Aryans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_Aryans "Indigenous Aryans") " and [Out of India theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Out_of_India_theory "Out of India theory"). Dating the Rig Veda as contemporaneous with (or even preceding) the [Indus Valley civilisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_Valley_civilisation "Indus Valley civilisation"), an argument is made that the IVC was Aryan, and the bearer of the Rig Veda.[^161] [^162] Indian nationalist [Bal Gangadhar Tilak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bal_Gangadhar_Tilak "Bal Gangadhar Tilak"), in his *Orion: Or Researches Into The Antiquity Of The Vedas* (1893) has concluded that the date of composition of the *Rigveda* dates at least as far back as 6000–4000 BCE based on his astronomical research into the position of the constellation [Orion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orion_\(constellation\) "Orion (constellation)").[^163] These theories are controversial, and not accepted or propagated in mainstream scholarship.[^164] [^165]

## Translations

The *Rigveda* is considered particularly difficult to translate, owing to its length, poetic nature, the language itself, and the absence of any close contemporary texts for comparison.[^166] [^167] Staal describes it as the most "obscure, distant and difficult for moderns to understand". As a result, he says, it "is often misinterpreted" – with many early translations containing straightforward errors – "or worse: used as a peg on which to hang an idea or a theory." [^168] [^144] Another issue is technical terms such as *[mandala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandala "Mandala")*, conventionally translated "book", but more literally rendered "cycle".[^144] [^169] Karen Thomson, author of a series of revisionary word studies and editor of the Metrically Restored Text Online at the University of Texas at Austin,[^170] argues, as linguists in the nineteenth century had done ([Friedrich Max Müller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_M%C3%BCller "Max Müller"), [Rudolf von Roth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolf_von_Roth "Rudolf von Roth"), [William Dwight Whitney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Dwight_Whitney "William Dwight Whitney"), [Theodor Benfey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodor_Benfey "Theodor Benfey"), [John Muir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Muir_\(indologist\) "John Muir (indologist)"), [Edward Vernon Arnold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Vernon_Arnold "Edward Vernon Arnold")), that the apparent obscurity derives from the failure to discard a mass of assumptions about ritual meaning inherited from Vedic tradition.[^171] [^172]

The first published translation of any portion of the *Rigveda* in any European language was into Latin, by [Friedrich August Rosen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_August_Rosen "Friedrich August Rosen"), working from manuscripts brought back from India by [Colebrooke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Thomas_Colebrooke "Henry Thomas Colebrooke"). In 1849, [Max Müller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_M%C3%BCller "Max Müller") published his six-volume translation into German, the first printed edition and most studied.[^173] [^174] [^12] [H. H. Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._H._Wilson "H. H. Wilson") was the first to make a translation of the Rig Veda into English, published from 1850–88.[^176] Wilson's version was based on a commentary of the complete text by [*Sāyaṇa*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayana "Sayana"), a 14th-century Sanskrit scholar, which he also translated.[^13]

Translations have since been made in several languages, including French and Russian.[^173] [Karl Friedrich Geldner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Friedrich_Geldner "Karl Friedrich Geldner") completed the first scholarly translation into German in the 1920s, which was published after his death.[^173] Translations of shorter cherrypicked anthologies have also been published, such as those by [Wendy Doniger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wendy_Doniger "Wendy Doniger") in 1981 and Walter Maurer in 1986, although Jamison and Brereton say they "tend to create a distorted view" of the text.[^173] In 1994, Barend A. van Nooten and Gary B. Holland published the first attempt to restore the entirety of the *Rigveda* to its poetic form, systematically identifying and correcting sound changes and [sandhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandhi "Sandhi") combinations which had distorted the original [metre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre_\(poetry\) "Metre (poetry)") and meaning.[^177] [^178]

It contains little else before and after this 'snapshot' view of contemporary Rgvedic history, as reported by these contemporary 'tape recordings.' On the other hand, the whole Rgvedic period may have lasted even up to 700 years, from the infiltration of the Indo-Aryans into the subcontinent, c. 1900 B.C. (at the utmost, the time of collapse of the Indus civilization), up to c. 1200 B.C., the time of the introduction of iron which is first mentioned in the clearly post-gvedic hymns of the Atharvaveda."
