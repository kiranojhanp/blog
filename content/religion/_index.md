**Religion** is a range of [social](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_system "Social system") - [cultural systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_system "Cultural system"), including designated [behaviors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_behaviour "Religious behaviour") and practices, [morals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morals "Morals"), [beliefs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belief "Belief"), [worldviews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worldview "Worldview"), [texts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_text "Religious text"), [sanctified places](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanctified_places "Sanctified places"), [prophecies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prophecies "Prophecies"), [ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics_in_religion "Ethics in religion"), or [organizations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_organization "Religious organization"), that generally relate humanity to [supernatural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supernatural "Supernatural"), [transcendental](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendence_(religion)> "Transcendence (religion)"), and [spiritual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spirituality "Spirituality") elements [^3] —although there is no scholarly consensus over what precisely constitutes a religion.[^4] [^5] It is an [essentially contested concept](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essentially_contested_concept "Essentially contested concept").[^6] Different religions may or may not contain various elements ranging from the [divine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine "Divine"),[^7] [sacredness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacredness "Sacredness"),[^8] [faith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faith "Faith"),[^9] and a supernatural being or beings.[^10]

The origin of religious belief is an open question, with possible explanations including awareness of individual death, a sense of community, and dreams.[^11] Religions have [sacred histories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacred_histories "Sacred histories"), [narratives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narrative "Narrative"), and [mythologies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mythologies "Mythologies"), preserved in oral traditions, [sacred texts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacred_texts "Sacred texts"), [symbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_symbol "Religious symbol"), and [holy places](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_places "Holy places"), that may attempt to explain the [origin of life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Origin_of_life "Origin of life"), the [universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_cosmology "Religious cosmology"), and other phenomena. Religious practice may include [rituals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ritual "Ritual"), [sermons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sermon "Sermon"), commemoration or veneration (of [deities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deities "Deities") or [saints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint "Saint")), [sacrifices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacrifice "Sacrifice"), [festivals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_festival "Religious festival"), [feasts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feast "Feast"), [trances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trance "Trance"), [initiations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initiation "Initiation"), [matrimonial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrimonial "Matrimonial") and [funerary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funerary "Funerary") services, [meditation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meditation "Meditation"), [prayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prayer "Prayer"), [music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_music "Religious music"), [art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_art "Religious art"), [dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_dance "Religious dance"), or [public service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_service "Public service").[^12]

There are an estimated 10,000 distinct religions worldwide,[^13] though nearly all of them have regionally based, relatively small followings. Four religions— [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity "Christianity"), [Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam "Islam"), [Hinduism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism "Hinduism"), and [Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism "Buddhism") —account for over 77% of the world's population, and 92% of the world either follows one of those four religions or identifies as [nonreligious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonreligious "Nonreligious"),[^14] meaning that the vast majority of remaining religions account for only 8% of the population combined. The religiously unaffiliated demographic includes those who do not identify with any particular religion, [atheists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheists "Atheists"), and [agnostics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agnostics "Agnostics"), although many in the demographic still have various religious beliefs.[^15] Many [world religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_religions "World religions") are also [organized religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organized_religion "Organized religion"), most definitively including the [Abrahamic religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abrahamic_religions "Abrahamic religions") Christianity, Islam, [Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judaism "Judaism"), and the [Baháʼí Faith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bah%C3%A1%CA%BC%C3%AD_Faith "Baháʼí Faith"), while others are arguably less so, in particular [folk religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folk_religion "Folk religion"), [indigenous religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_religion "Indigenous religion"), and some [Eastern religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_religions "Eastern religions"). A portion of the world's population are members of [new religious movements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_religious_movement "New religious movement").[^16] Scholars have indicated that [global religiosity may be increasing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desecularization "Desecularization") due to religious countries having generally higher birth rates.[^17]

The [study of religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Study_of_religion "Study of religion") comprises a wide variety of academic disciplines, including [theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theology "Theology"), [philosophy of religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_religion "Philosophy of religion"), [comparative religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion "Comparative religion"), and social scientific studies. [Theories about religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theories_about_religion "Theories about religion") offer various explanations for its origins and workings, including the [ontological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontological "Ontological") foundations of religious [being](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Being "Being") and belief.[^18]

### Etymology

The term _religion_ comes from both [Old French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_French "Old French") and [Anglo-Norman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Norman_language "Anglo-Norman language") (1200s [CE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Era "Common Era")) and means respect for sense of right, moral obligation, sanctity, what is sacred, reverence for the gods.[^19] [^20] It is ultimately derived from the [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin "Latin") word _[religiō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religio "Religio")_. According to Roman philosopher [Cicero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cicero "Cicero"), _religiō_ comes from _relegere_: _re_ (meaning 'again') + _lego_ (meaning 'read'), where _lego_ is in the sense of 'go over', 'choose', or 'consider carefully'. Contrarily, some modern scholars such as [Tom Harpur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Harpur "Tom Harpur") and [Joseph Campbell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Campbell "Joseph Campbell") have argued that _religiō_ is derived from _religare_: _re_ (meaning 'again') + _ligare_ ('bind' or 'connect'), which was made prominent by [St. Augustine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustine_of_Hippo "Augustine of Hippo") following the interpretation given by [Lactantius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactantius "Lactantius") in _Divinae institutiones_, IV, 28.[^21] [^22] The medieval usage alternates with _order_ in designating bonded communities like those of [monastic orders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monastic_orders "Monastic orders"): "we hear of the 'religion' of the [Golden Fleece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_the_Golden_Fleece "Order of the Golden Fleece"), of a knight 'of the [religion of Avys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_Aviz "Order of Aviz") '." [^23]

#### Religiō

In classic antiquity, _religiō_ broadly meant [conscientiousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conscientiousness "Conscientiousness"), sense of [right](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Righteousness "Righteousness"), moral [obligation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obligation "Obligation"), or [duty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duty "Duty") to anything.[^24] In the ancient and medieval world, the etymological Latin root _religiō_ was understood as an individual virtue of [worship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worship "Worship") in mundane contexts; never as [doctrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctrine "Doctrine"), practice, or actual source of [knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knowledge "Knowledge").[^25] [^26] In general, _religiō_ referred to broad social obligations towards anything including family, neighbors, rulers, and even towards [God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God "God").[^27] _Religiō_ was most often used by the [ancient Romans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Romans "Ancient Romans") not in the context of a relation towards gods, but as a range of general emotions which arose from heightened attention in any mundane context such as [hesitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hesitation "Hesitation"), caution, [anxiety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety "Anxiety"), or [fear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fear "Fear"), as well as feelings of being bound, restricted, or inhibited.[^28] The term was also closely related to other terms like _scrupulus_ (which meant "very precisely"), and some Roman authors related the term _superstitio_ (which meant too much fear or anxiety or shame) to _religiō_ at times.[^28] When _religiō_ came into [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language "English language") around the 1200s as religion, it took the meaning of "life bound by monastic vows" or monastic orders.[^23] [^27] The compartmentalized concept of religion, where religious and [worldly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worldly "Worldly") things were separated, was not used before the 1500s.[^27] The concept of religion was first used in the 1500s to distinguish the domain of the [church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church "Catholic Church") and the domain of ; the [Peace of Augsburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_of_Augsburg "Peace of Augsburg") marks such an instance,[^27] which has been described by [Christian Reus-Smit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Reus-Smit "Christian Reus-Smit") as "the first step on the road toward a European system of [sovereign states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sovereign_state "Sovereign state")." [^29]

Roman general [Julius Caesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Caesar "Julius Caesar") used _religiō_ to mean 'obligation of an oath' when discussing captured soldiers making an oath to their captors.[^30] Roman naturalist [Pliny the Elder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pliny_the_Elder "Pliny the Elder") used the term _religiō_ to describe the apparent respect given by elephants to the [night sky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Night_sky "Night sky").[^31] Cicero used _religiō_ as being related to _cultum deorum_ (worship of the gods).[^32]

#### Threskeia

In [Ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece "Ancient Greece"), the Greek term _threskeia_ (θρησκεία) was loosely translated into Latin as _religiō_ in [late antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_antiquity "Late antiquity"). _Threskeia_ was sparsely used in classical Greece but became more frequently used in the writings of [Josephus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josephus "Josephus") in the 1st century CE. It was used in mundane contexts and could mean multiple things from respectful fear to excessive or harmfully distracting practices of others, to cultic practices. It was often contrasted with the Greek word _deisidaimonia_, which meant too much fear.[^33]

Religion is a modern concept.[^34] And is not a universal concept across history, cultures or languages.[^35] [^36] The concept was invented recently in the English language and is found in texts from the 17th century due to events such as the splitting of [Christendom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christendom "Christendom") during the [Protestant Reformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant_Reformation "Protestant Reformation") and [globalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globalization "Globalization") in the [Age of Exploration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Exploration "Age of Exploration"), which involved contact with numerous foreign cultures with non-European languages.[^25] [^26] [^37] Some argue that regardless of its definition, it is not appropriate to apply the term religion to non-Western cultures,[^38] [^39] while some followers of various faiths rebuke using the word to describe their own belief system.[^40]

The concept of "ancient religion" stems from modern interpretations of a range of practices that conform to a modern concept of religion, influenced by early modern and 19th century Christian discourse.[^41] The concept of religion was formed in the 16th and 17th centuries,[^42] [^43] despite the fact that ancient sacred texts like the [Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible "Bible"), the [Quran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quran "Quran"), and others did not have a word or even a concept of religion in the original languages and neither did the people or the cultures in which these sacred texts were written.[^44] [^45] For example, there is no precise equivalent of religion in Hebrew, and [Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judaism "Judaism") does not distinguish clearly between religious, national, racial, or ethnic identities.[^46] [^47] [^48] One of its central concepts is _[halakha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halakha "Halakha")_, meaning the walk or path sometimes translated as law, which guides religious practice and belief and many aspects of daily life.[^49] Even though the beliefs and traditions of Judaism are found in the ancient world, ancient Jews saw [Jewish identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_identity "Jewish identity") as being about an ethnic or national identity and did not entail a compulsory belief system or regulated rituals.[^50] In the 1st century CE, Josephus had used the Greek term _ioudaismos_ (Judaism) as an ethnic term and was not linked to modern abstract concepts of religion or a set of beliefs.[^5] The very concept of "Judaism" was invented by the [Christian Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Church "Christian Church"),[^51] and it was in the 19th century that Jews began to see their ancestral culture as a religion analogous to Christianity.[^50] The Greek word _threskeia_, which was used by Greek writers such as [Herodotus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herodotus "Herodotus") and Josephus, is found in the [New Testament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Testament "New Testament"). _Threskeia_ is sometimes translated as "religion" in today's translations, but the term was understood as generic "worship" well into the [medieval period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_period "Medieval period").[^5] In the Quran, the [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic "Arabic") word _[din](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Din_(Arabic)> "Din (Arabic)")_ is often translated as religion in modern translations, but up to the mid-1600s translators expressed _din_ as "law." [^5]

The [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit "Sanskrit") word [dharma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharma "Dharma"), sometimes translated as religion,[^52] also means law. Throughout classical [South Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Asia "South Asia"), the [study of law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharma%C5%9B%C4%81stra "Dharmaśāstra") consisted of concepts such as [penance through piety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pr%C4%81ya%C5%9Bcitta "Prāyaścitta") and [ceremonial as well as practical traditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%80c%C4%81ra "Ācāra"). Medieval Japan at first had a similar union between imperial law and universal or Buddha law, but these later became independent sources of power.[^53] [^54]

Though traditions, sacred texts, and practices have existed throughout time, most cultures did not align with Western conceptions of religion since they did not separate everyday life from the sacred. In the 18th and 19th centuries, the terms Buddhism, Hinduism, Taoism, Confucianism, and [world religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_religions "World religions") first entered the English language.[^55] [^56] [^57] Native Americans were also thought of as not having religions and also had no word for religion in their languages either.[^56] [^58] No one self-identified as a Hindu or Buddhist or other similar terms before the 1800s.[^59] "Hindu" has historically been used as a geographical, cultural, and later religious identifier for people indigenous to the [Indian subcontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_subcontinent "Indian subcontinent").[^60] [^61] Throughout its long history, Japan had no concept of religion since there was no corresponding Japanese word, nor anything close to its meaning, but when [American warships appeared off the coast of Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perry_Expedition "Perry Expedition") in 1853 and forced the Japanese government to sign treaties demanding, among other things, [freedom of religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_religion "Freedom of religion"), the country had to contend with this idea.[^62] [^63]

According to the [philologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philologist "Philologist") [Max Müller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_M%C3%BCller "Max Müller") in the 19th century, the root of the English word religion, the Latin _religiō_, was originally used to mean only reverence for God or the gods, careful pondering of divine things, [piety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pietas "Pietas") (which Cicero further derived to mean diligence).[^64] [^65] Müller characterized many other cultures around the world, including Egypt, Persia, and India, as having a similar power structure at this point in history. What is called ancient religion today, they would have only called law.[^66]

Accordingly, other concepts like the "secular", "nonreligion", and "atheism" have been called into question since they are, like religion, not universal concepts as they are not found in many other cultures.[^67] Anthropoligically, this is also the case where Western terms and concepts like "religion" and "secular" do not exist in other cultures.[^68] Sociologists and demographers have noted that outside the West, concepts of "religion" or "the secular" are not always rooted in local culture and may not even be present.[^69] Other studies indicate that religion and nonreligion are not necessarily mutually exclusive experiences since there is overlap in individuals regular lives.[^70] [^71]

## Definition

Scholars have failed to agree on a definition of religion. There are, however, two general definition systems: the sociological/functional and the phenomenological/philosophical.[^72] [^73] [^74] [^75]

### Modern Western

The concept of religion originated in the [modern era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_era "Modern era") in the [West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_culture "Western culture").[^39] Parallel concepts are not found in many current and past cultures; there is no equivalent term for religion in many languages.[^5] [^27] Scholars have found it difficult to develop a consistent definition, with some giving up on the possibility of a definition.[^76] [^77] Others argue that regardless of its definition, it is not appropriate to apply it to non-Western cultures.[^38] [^39]

An increasing number of scholars have expressed reservations about ever defining the essence of religion.[^78] They observe that the way the concept today is used is a particularly modern construct that would not have been understood through much of history and in many cultures outside the West (or even in the West until after the [Peace of Westphalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_of_Westphalia "Peace of Westphalia")).[^79] The MacMillan Encyclopedia of Religions states:

> The very attempt to define religion, to find some distinctive or possibly unique essence or set of qualities that distinguish the religious from the remainder of human life, is primarily a Western concern. The attempt is a natural consequence of the Western speculative, intellectualistic, and scientific disposition. It is also the product of the dominant Western religious mode, what is called the Judeo-Christian climate or, more accurately, the theistic inheritance from Judaism, Christianity, and Islam. The theistic form of belief in this tradition, even when downgraded culturally, is formative of the [dichotomous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dichotomy "Dichotomy") Western view of religion. That is, the basic structure of theism is essentially a distinction between a transcendent deity and all else, between the creator and his creation, between God and man.[^80]

The anthropologist [Clifford Geertz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clifford_Geertz "Clifford Geertz") defined religion as a:

> ... system of symbols which acts to establish powerful, pervasive, and long-lasting moods and motivations in men by formulating conceptions of a general order of existence and clothing these conceptions with such an aura of factuality that the moods and motivations seem uniquely realistic.[^81]

Alluding perhaps to Tylor's "deeper motive", Geertz remarked that:

> ... we have very little idea of how, in empirical terms, this particular miracle is accomplished. We just know that it is done, annually, weekly, daily, for some people almost hourly; and we have an enormous ethnographic literature to demonstrate it.[^82]

The theologian [Antoine Vergote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoine_Vergote "Antoine Vergote") took the term supernatural simply to mean whatever transcends the powers of nature or human agency. He also emphasized the cultural reality of religion, which he defined as:

> ... the entirety of the linguistic expressions, emotions and, actions and signs that refer to a supernatural being or supernatural beings.[^10]

[Peter Mandaville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Mandaville "Peter Mandaville") and [Paul James](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_James_(academic)> "Paul James (academic)") intended to get away from the modernist dualisms or dichotomous understandings of immanence/transcendence, spirituality/materialism, and sacredness/secularity. They define religion as:

> ... a relatively-bounded system of beliefs, symbols and practices that addresses the nature of existence, and in which communion with others and Otherness is _lived_ as if it both takes in and spiritually transcends socially-grounded ontologies of time, space, embodiment and knowing.[^83]

According to the _MacMillan Encyclopedia of Religions_, there is an experiential aspect to religion which can be found in almost every culture:

> ... almost every known culture \[has\] a depth dimension in cultural experiences... toward some sort of ultimacy and transcendence that will provide norms and power for the rest of life. When more or less distinct patterns of behavior are built around this depth dimension in a culture, this structure constitutes religion in its historically recognizable form. Religion is the organization of life around the depth dimensions of experience—varied in form, completeness, and clarity in accordance with the environing culture.[^84]

Anthropologists Lyle Steadman and Craig T. Palmer emphasized the communication of supernatural beliefs, defining religion as:

> ... the communicated acceptance by individuals of another individual's "supernatural" claim, a claim whose accuracy is not verifiable by the senses.[^85]

### Classical

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/df/%D0%91%D1%83%D0%B4%D0%B0%D0%B6%D0%B0%D0%BF_%D0%A6%D1%8B%D1%80%D0%B5%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%BE%D0%B2.JPG/500px-%D0%91%D1%83%D0%B4%D0%B0%D0%B6%D0%B0%D0%BF_%D0%A6%D1%8B%D1%80%D0%B5%D1%82%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%BE%D0%B2.JPG)

Budazhap Shiretorov (Будажап Цыреторов), the head shaman of the religious community Altan Serge (Алтан Сэргэ) in Buryatia

[Friedrich Schleiermacher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Schleiermacher "Friedrich Schleiermacher") in the late 18th century defined religion as _das schlechthinnige Abhängigkeitsgefühl_, commonly translated as "the feeling of absolute dependence".[^86]

His contemporary [Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Wilhelm_Friedrich_Hegel "Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel") disagreed thoroughly, defining religion as "the Divine Spirit becoming conscious of Himself through the finite spirit." [^87]

[Edward Burnett Tylor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Burnett_Tylor "Edward Burnett Tylor") defined religion in 1871 as "the belief in spiritual beings".[^88] He argued that narrowing the definition to mean the belief in a supreme deity or judgment after death or [idolatry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idolatry "Idolatry") and so on, would exclude many peoples from the category of religious, and thus "has the fault of identifying religion rather with particular developments than with the deeper motive which underlies them." He also argued that the belief in spiritual beings exists in all known societies.

In his book _[The Varieties of Religious Experience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Varieties_of_Religious_Experience "The Varieties of Religious Experience")_, the psychologist [William James](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_James "William James") defined religion as "the feelings, acts, and experiences of individual men in their solitude, so far as they apprehend themselves to stand in relation to whatever they may consider the divine." [^7] By the term divine James meant "any object that is god _like_, whether it be a concrete deity or not" [^89] to which the individual feels impelled to respond with solemnity and gravity.[^90]

Sociologist [Émile Durkheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89mile_Durkheim "Émile Durkheim"), in his seminal book _[The Elementary Forms of the Religious Life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Elementary_Forms_of_the_Religious_Life "The Elementary Forms of the Religious Life")_, defined religion as a "unified system of beliefs and practices relative to sacred things".[^8] By sacred things he meant things "set apart and forbidden—beliefs and practices which unite into one single moral community called a Church, all those who adhere to them." [Sacred things](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacred "Sacred") are not, however, limited to gods or spirits.[^1] On the contrary, a sacred thing can be "a rock, a tree, a spring, a pebble, a piece of wood, a house, in a word, anything can be sacred." [^91] Religious beliefs, myths, dogmas and legends are the representations that express the nature of these sacred things, and the virtues and powers which are attributed to them.[^92]

Echoes of James' and Durkheim's definitions are to be found in the writings of, for example, [Frederick Ferré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Ferr%C3%A9 "Frederick Ferré") who defined religion as "one's way of valuing most comprehensively and intensively".[^93] Similarly, for the theologian [Paul Tillich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Tillich "Paul Tillich"), faith is "the state of being ultimately concerned",[^9] which "is itself religion. Religion is the substance, the ground, and the depth of man's spiritual life." [^94]

When religion is seen in terms of sacred, divine, intensive valuing, or ultimate concern, then it is possible to understand why scientific findings and philosophical criticisms (e.g., those made by [Richard Dawkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Dawkins "Richard Dawkins")) do not necessarily disturb its adherents.[^95]

## Aspects

### Beliefs

The origin of religious belief is an open question, with possible explanations including awareness of individual death, a sense of community, and dreams.[^11] Traditionally, [faith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faith "Faith"), in addition to [reason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reason "Reason"), has been considered a source of religious beliefs. The interplay between faith and reason, and their use as perceived support for religious beliefs, have been a subject of interest to philosophers and theologians.[^96]

### Mythology

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/81/Kurukshetra.jpg/500px-Kurukshetra.jpg)

A manuscript depicting the climactic Kurukshetra War in Hindu epic Mahabharata. The is the longest epic poem known and a key source of Hindu mythology.

The word _myth_ has several meanings:

1. A traditional story of ostensibly historical events that serves to unfold part of the world view of a people or explain a practice, belief, or natural phenomenon;
2. A person or thing having only an imaginary or unverifiable existence; or
3. A metaphor for the spiritual potentiality in the human being.[^97]

Ancient [polytheistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytheistic "Polytheistic") religions, such as those of Greece, [Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome "Ancient Rome"), and [Scandinavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scandinavia "Scandinavia"), are usually categorized under the heading of [mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mythology "Mythology"). Religions of pre-industrial peoples, or [cultures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture "Culture") in development, are similarly called myths in the [anthropology of religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropology_of_religion "Anthropology of religion"). The term myth can be used pejoratively by both religious and non-religious people. By defining another person's religious stories and beliefs as mythology, one implies that they are less real or true than one's own religious stories and beliefs. [Joseph Campbell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Campbell "Joseph Campbell") remarked, "Mythology is often thought of as _other people's_ religions, and religion can be defined as misinterpreted mythology." [^98]

In sociology, however, the term myth has a non-pejorative meaning. There, myth is defined as a story that is important for the group, whether or not it is objectively or provably true.[^99] Examples include the [resurrection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resurrection "Resurrection") of their real-life founder [Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus "Jesus"), which, to Christians, explains the means by which they are freed from sin, is symbolic of the power of life over death, and is also said to be a historical event. But from a mythological outlook, whether or not the event actually occurred is unimportant. Instead, the [symbolism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbol "Symbol") of the death of an old life and the start of a new life is most significant. Religious believers may or may not accept such symbolic interpretations.

### Practices

The practices of a religion may include [rituals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ritual "Ritual"), [sermons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sermon "Sermon"), commemoration or veneration of a [deity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deity "Deity") (god or [goddess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goddess "Goddess")), [sacrifices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacrifice "Sacrifice"), [festivals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Festival "Festival"), [feasts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banquet "Banquet"), [trances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trance "Trance"), [initiations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initiation "Initiation"), [funerary services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funeral "Funeral"), [matrimonial services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrimony "Matrimony"), [meditation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meditation "Meditation"), [prayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prayer "Prayer"), [religious music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_music "Religious music"), [religious art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_art "Religious art"), [sacred dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacred_dance "Sacred dance"), [public service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Community_service "Community service"), or other aspects of human culture.[^100]

### Social organisation

Religions have a societal basis, either as a living tradition which is carried by lay participants, or with an organized [clergy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clergy "Clergy"), and a definition of what constitutes adherence or membership.

## Academic study

A number of disciplines study the phenomenon of religion: [theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theology "Theology"), [comparative religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion "Comparative religion"), [history of religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_religion "History of religion"), [evolutionary origin of religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_origin_of_religions "Evolutionary origin of religions"), [anthropology of religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropology_of_religion "Anthropology of religion"), [psychology of religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology_of_religion "Psychology of religion") (including [neuroscience of religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurotheology "Neurotheology") and [evolutionary psychology of religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_psychology_of_religion "Evolutionary psychology of religion")), [law and religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_and_religion "Law and religion"), and [sociology of religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociology_of_religion "Sociology of religion").

Daniel L. Pals mentions eight classical theories of religion, focusing on various aspects of religion: [animism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animism "Animism") and [magic](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magic_(paranormal)> "Magic (paranormal)"), by [E.B. Tylor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Burnett_Tylor "Edward Burnett Tylor") and [J.G. Frazer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J.G._Frazer "J.G. Frazer"); the [psycho-analytic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychoanalysis "Psychoanalysis") approach of [Sigmund Freud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigmund_Freud "Sigmund Freud"); and further [Émile Durkheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89mile_Durkheim "Émile Durkheim"), [Karl Marx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Marx "Karl Marx"), [Max Weber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Weber "Max Weber"), [Mircea Eliade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mircea_Eliade "Mircea Eliade"), [E.E. Evans-Pritchard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E.E._Evans-Pritchard "E.E. Evans-Pritchard"), and [Clifford Geertz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clifford_Geertz "Clifford Geertz").[^101]

[Michael Stausberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Stausberg "Michael Stausberg") gives an overview of contemporary theories of religion, including [cognitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_science_of_religion "Cognitive science of religion") and biological approaches.[^102]

### Theories

[Sociological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociology_of_religion "Sociology of religion") and [anthropological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropology_of_religion "Anthropology of religion") theories of religion generally attempt to explain the [origin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Origin_of_religion "Origin of religion") and [function of religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_function "Social function").[^103] These theories define what they present as universal characteristics of [religious belief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_belief "Religious belief") and [practice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_behaviour "Religious behaviour").

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/de/Yazilikaya_B_12erGruppe.jpg/500px-Yazilikaya_B_12erGruppe.jpg)

The Yazılıkaya sanctuary in Turkey, with the twelve gods of the underworld

The origin of religion is uncertain. There are a number of theories regarding the subsequent origins of religious practices.

According to [anthropologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropologists "Anthropologists") John Monaghan and Peter Just, "Many of the great world religions appear to have begun as revitalization movements of some sort, as the vision of a charismatic prophet fires the imaginations of people seeking a more comprehensive answer to their problems than they feel is provided by everyday beliefs. Charismatic individuals have emerged at many times and places in the world. It seems that the key to long-term success—and many movements come and go with little long-term effect—has relatively little to do with the prophets, who appear with surprising regularity, but more to do with the development of a group of supporters who are able to institutionalize the movement." [^104]

The [development of religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Development_of_religion "Development of religion") has taken different forms in different cultures. Some religions place an emphasis on belief, while others emphasize practice. Some religions focus on the subjective experience of the religious individual, while others consider the activities of the religious community to be most important. Some religions claim to be universal, believing their [laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law "Law") and [cosmology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmology "Cosmology") to be binding for everyone, while others are intended to be practiced only by a closely defined or localized group. In many places, religion has been associated with public institutions such as [education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education "Education"), [hospitals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hospital "Hospital"), the [family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Family "Family"), [government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government "Government"), and [political](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politics "Politics") hierarchies.[^105]

Anthropologists John Monoghan and Peter Just state that, "it seems apparent that one thing religion or belief helps us do is deal with problems of human life that are significant, persistent, and intolerable. One important way in which religious beliefs accomplish this is by providing a set of ideas about how and why the world is put together that allows people to accommodate anxieties and deal with misfortune." [^105]

#### Cultural system

While religion is difficult to define, one standard model of religion, used in [religious studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_studies "Religious studies") courses, was proposed by [Clifford Geertz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clifford_Geertz "Clifford Geertz"), who simply called it a "cultural system".[^106] A critique of Geertz's model by [Talal Asad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talal_Asad "Talal Asad") categorized religion as "an [anthropological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropology "Anthropology") category".[^107] Richard Niebuhr's (1894–1962) five-fold classification of the relationship between Christ and culture, however, indicates that religion and culture can be seen as two separate systems, though with some interplay.[^108]

#### Social constructionism

One modern academic theory of religion, [social constructionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_constructionism "Social constructionism"), says that religion is a modern concept that suggests all [spiritual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spirituality "Spirituality") practice and [worship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worship "Worship") follows a model similar to the [Abrahamic religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abrahamic_religions "Abrahamic religions") as an orientation system that helps to interpret reality and define human beings.[^109] Among the main proponents of this theory of religion are Daniel Dubuisson, Timothy Fitzgerald, Talal Asad, and Jason Ānanda Josephson. The social constructionists argue that religion is a modern concept that developed from Christianity and was then applied inappropriately to non-Western cultures.

#### Cognitive science

Cognitive science of religion is the study of religious thought and behavior from the perspective of the cognitive and evolutionary sciences.[^110] The field employs methods and theories from a very broad range of disciplines, including: [cognitive psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_psychology "Cognitive psychology"), [evolutionary psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_psychology "Evolutionary psychology"), [cognitive anthropology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_anthropology "Cognitive anthropology"), [artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence "Artificial intelligence"), [cognitive neuroscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_neuroscience "Cognitive neuroscience"), [neurobiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurobiology "Neurobiology"), [zoology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoology "Zoology"), and [ethology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethology "Ethology"). Scholars in this field seek to explain how human minds acquire, generate, and transmit religious thoughts, practices, and schemas by means of ordinary cognitive capacities.

[Hallucinations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hallucination "Hallucination") and [delusions related to religious content](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_delusion "Religious delusion") occurs in about 60% of people with [schizophrenia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schizophrenia "Schizophrenia"). While this number varies across cultures, this had led to theories about a number of influential religious phenomena and possible relation to psychotic disorders. A number of prophetic experiences are consistent with psychotic symptoms, although [retrospective diagnoses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retrospective_diagnosis "Retrospective diagnosis") are practically impossible.[^111] [^112] [^113] Schizophrenic episodes are also experienced by people who do not have belief in gods.[^114]

Religious content is also common in [temporal lobe epilepsy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temporal_lobe_epilepsy "Temporal lobe epilepsy"), and [obsessive-compulsive disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obsessive%E2%80%93compulsive_disorder "Obsessive–compulsive disorder").[^115] [^116] [^117] Atheistic content is also found to be common with temporal lobe epilepsy.[^118]

### Comparativism

Comparative religion is the branch of the [study of religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Study_of_religions "Study of religions") concerned with the systematic comparison of the doctrines and practices of the world's religions. In general, the comparative study of religion yields a deeper understanding of the fundamental philosophical concerns of religion such as [ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics "Ethics"), [metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics "Metaphysics"), and the nature and form of [salvation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salvation "Salvation"). Studying such material is meant to give one a richer and more sophisticated understanding of human beliefs and practices regarding the [sacred](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacred "Sacred"), [numinous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numinous "Numinous"), [spiritual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spirituality "Spirituality") and [divine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divinity "Divinity").[^119]

In the field of comparative religion, a common geographical classification [^120] of the [main world religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_religious_groups "Major religious groups") includes [Middle Eastern religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Eastern_religions "Middle Eastern religions") (including [Zoroastrianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroastrianism "Zoroastrianism") and [Iranian religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_religions "Iranian religions")), [Indian religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_religions "Indian religions"), [East Asian religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Asian_religions "East Asian religions"), African religions, American religions, Oceanic religions, and classical Hellenistic religions.[^120]

## Classification

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0c/Prevailing_religious_population_by_country_percentage.svg/750px-Prevailing_religious_population_by_country_percentage.svg.png)

A map of major denominations and religions of the world

In the 19th and 20th centuries, the academic practice of [comparative religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion "Comparative religion") divided religious belief into philosophically defined categories called world religions. Some academics [studying the subject](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Study_of_religion "Study of religion") have divided religions into three broad categories:

1. [World religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_religions "World religions"), a term which refers to [transcultural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transculturation "Transculturation"), international religions;
2. [Indigenous religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_religions "Indigenous religions"), which refers to smaller, culture-specific or nation-specific religious groups; and
3. [New religious movements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_religious_movements "New religious movements"), which refers to recently developed religions.[^121]

Some recent scholarship has argued that not all types of religion are necessarily separated by mutually exclusive philosophies, and furthermore that the utility of ascribing a practice to a certain philosophy, or even calling a given practice religious, rather than cultural, political, or social in nature, is limited.[^122] [^123] [^124] The current state of psychological study about the nature of religiousness suggests that it is better to refer to religion as a largely invariant phenomenon that should be distinguished from cultural norms (i.e. religions).[^125]

### Morphological classification

Some [religion scholars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_studies "Religious studies") classify religions as either _[universal religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universalizing_religion "Universalizing religion")_ that seek worldwide acceptance and actively look for new [converts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_conversion "Religious conversion"), such as the Baháʼí Faith, Buddhism, Christianity, Islam, and Jainism, while _[ethnic religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_religion "Ethnic religion")_ are identified with a particular ethnic group and do not seek converts.[^126] [^127] Others reject the distinction, pointing out that all religious practices, whatever their philosophical origin, are ethnic because they come from a particular culture.[^128] [^129] [^130]

A global poll in 2012 surveyed 57 countries and reported that 59% of the world's population identified as religious, 23% as [not religious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irreligion "Irreligion"), 13% as convinced [atheists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheism "Atheism"), and also a 9% decrease in identification as religious when compared to the 2005 average from 39 countries.[^132] A follow-up poll in 2015 found that 63% of the globe identified as religious, 22% as not religious, and 11% as convinced atheists.[^133] On average, women are more religious than men.[^134] Some people follow multiple religions or multiple religious principles at the same time, regardless of whether or not the religious principles they follow traditionally allow for [syncretism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syncretism "Syncretism").[^135] [^136] [^137] Unaffiliated populations are projected to drop, even when taking disaffiliation rates into account, due to differences in birth rates.[^138] [^139]

Scholars have indicated that [global religiosity may be increasing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desecularization "Desecularization") due to religious countries having higher birth rates in general.[^140]

## Specific religions

### Abrahamic

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/62/Moln%C3%A1r_%C3%81brah%C3%A1m_kik%C3%B6lt%C3%B6z%C3%A9se_1850.jpg/500px-Moln%C3%A1r_%C3%81brah%C3%A1m_kik%C3%B6lt%C3%B6z%C3%A9se_1850.jpg)

The patriarch Abraham (by József Molnár )

[Abrahamic religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abrahamic_religions "Abrahamic religions") are [monotheistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotheistic "Monotheistic") religions which believe they descend from [Abraham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham "Abraham").

#### Judaism

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/dd/Open_Torah_and_pointer.jpg/500px-Open_Torah_and_pointer.jpg)

The Torah is the primary sacred text of Judaism.

[Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judaism "Judaism") is the oldest Abrahamic religion, originating in the people of [ancient Israel and Judah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_ancient_Israel_and_Judah "History of ancient Israel and Judah").[^141] The [Torah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torah "Torah") is its foundational text, and is part of the larger text known as the [Tanakh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanakh "Tanakh") or [Hebrew Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_Bible "Hebrew Bible"). It is supplemented by oral tradition, set down in written form in later texts such as the [Midrash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midrash "Midrash") and the [Talmud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talmud "Talmud"). Judaism includes a wide corpus of texts, practices, theological positions, and forms of organization. Within Judaism there are a variety of movements, most of which emerged from [Rabbinic Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabbinic_Judaism "Rabbinic Judaism"), which holds that God revealed his laws and [commandments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/613_Mitzvot "613 Mitzvot") to [Moses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moses "Moses") on [Mount Sinai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biblical_Mount_Sinai "Biblical Mount Sinai") in the form of both the [Written](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torah "Torah") and [Oral Torah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oral_Torah "Oral Torah"); historically, this assertion was challenged by various groups. The [Jewish people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_people "Jewish people") were scattered after the destruction of the [Temple in Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_in_Jerusalem "Temple in Jerusalem") in 70 CE. Today there are about 13 million Jews, about 40 per cent living in Israel and 40 per cent in the United States.[^142] The largest [Jewish religious movements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_religious_movements "Jewish religious movements") are [Orthodox Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthodox_Judaism "Orthodox Judaism") ([Haredi Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haredi_Judaism "Haredi Judaism") and [Modern Orthodox Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_Orthodox_Judaism "Modern Orthodox Judaism")), [Conservative Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative_Judaism "Conservative Judaism") and [Reform Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reform_Judaism "Reform Judaism").[^141]

#### Christianity

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/29/ISR-2013-Jerusalem-Holy_Sepulchre-dome.jpg/500px-ISR-2013-Jerusalem-Holy_Sepulchre-dome.jpg)

Jesus is the central figure of Christianity.

[Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity "Christianity") is based on the life and teachings of [Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus "Jesus") of Nazareth (1st century) as presented in the New Testament.[^143] The Christian faith is essentially faith in Jesus as the [Christ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christ "Christ"),[^143] the [Son of God](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Son_of_God_(Christianity)> "Son of God (Christianity)"), and as [Savior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messiah "Messiah") and Lord. Almost all Christians believe in the [Trinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity "Trinity"), which teaches the unity of [Father](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_the_Father "God the Father"), [Son](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_the_Son "God the Son") (Jesus Christ), and [Holy Spirit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Spirit_in_Christianity "Holy Spirit in Christianity") as three persons in [one Godhead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Godhead_in_Christianity "Godhead in Christianity"). Most Christians can describe their faith with the [Nicene Creed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicene_Creed "Nicene Creed"). As the religion of [Byzantine Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire "Byzantine Empire") in the first millennium and of [Western Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Europe "Western Europe") during the time of colonization, Christianity has been propagated throughout the world via [missionary work](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_mission "Christian mission").[^144] [^145] [^146] It is the [world's largest religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_religious_groups "Major religious groups"), with about 2.3 billion followers as of 2015.[^147] The main divisions of Christianity are, according to the number of adherents:[^148]

- The [Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church "Catholic Church"), led by the [Bishop of Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_of_Rome "Bishop of Rome") and the bishops worldwide in communion with him, is a [communion](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communion_(Christian)> "Communion (Christian)") of 24 Churches _[sui iuris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sui_iuris "Sui iuris")_, including the [Latin Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_Church "Latin Church") and 23 [Eastern Catholic churches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Catholic_Churches "Eastern Catholic Churches"), such as the [Maronite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maronite "Maronite") Catholic Church.[^148]
- [Eastern Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Christianity "Eastern Christianity"), which include [Eastern Orthodoxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodoxy "Eastern Orthodoxy"), [Oriental Orthodoxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriental_Orthodoxy "Oriental Orthodoxy"), and the [Church of the East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_the_East "Church of the East").
- [Protestantism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism "Protestantism"), separated from the Catholic Church in the 16th-century [Protestant Reformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant_Reformation "Protestant Reformation") and is split into thousands of [denominations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_denomination "Religious denomination"). Major branches of Protestantism include [Anglicanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglicanism "Anglicanism"), [Baptists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baptists "Baptists"), [Calvinism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvinism "Calvinism"), [Lutheranism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lutheranism "Lutheranism"), and [Methodism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methodism "Methodism"), though each of these contain many different denominations or groups.[^148]

There are also smaller groups, including:

- [Restorationism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restorationism "Restorationism"), the belief that Christianity should be restored (as opposed to reformed) along the lines of what is known about the [apostolic early church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apostolic_Age "Apostolic Age").
- [Latter-day Saint movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latter-day_Saint_movement "Latter-day Saint movement"), founded by [Joseph Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Smith "Joseph Smith") in the late 1820s.
- [Jehovah's Witnesses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jehovah%27s_Witnesses "Jehovah's Witnesses"), founded in the late 1870s by [Charles Taze Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Taze_Russell "Charles Taze Russell").

#### Islam

[Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam "Islam") is a [monotheistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotheism "Monotheism") [^149] religion based on the [Quran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quran "Quran"),[^149] one of the [holy books](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_holy_books "Islamic holy books") considered by Muslims to be [revealed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wahy "Wahy") by [God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_in_Islam "God in Islam"), and on the [teachings (hadith)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadith "Hadith") of the [Islamic prophet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prophets_of_Islam "Prophets of Islam") [Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad "Muhammad"), a major political and religious figure of the 7th century CE. Islam is based on the unity of all religious philosophies and accepts all of the [Abrahamic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abrahamic_religions "Abrahamic religions") prophets of Judaism, Christianity and other Abrahamic religions before [Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad "Muhammad"). It is the most widely practiced religion of [Southeast Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Asia "Southeast Asia"), [North Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Africa "North Africa"), [Western Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Asia "Western Asia"), and [Central Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Asia "Central Asia"), while [Muslim-majority countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim-majority_countries "Muslim-majority countries") also exist in parts of [South Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Asia "South Asia"), [Sub-Saharan Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sub-Saharan_Africa "Sub-Saharan Africa"), and [Southeast Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Europe "Southeast Europe"). There are also several [Islamic republics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_republic "Islamic republic"), including [Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran "Iran"), [Pakistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistan "Pakistan"), [Mauritania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mauritania "Mauritania"), and [Afghanistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afghanistan "Afghanistan"). With about 1.8 billion followers (2015), almost a quarter of [earth's population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_population "World population") are [Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslims "Muslims").[^150]

- [Sunni Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam "Sunni Islam") is the largest denomination within Islam and follows the Qur'an, the ahadith (plural of Hadith) which record the [sunnah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunnah "Sunnah"), whilst placing emphasis on the [sahabah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahabah "Sahabah").
- [Shia Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shia_Islam "Shia Islam") is the second largest denomination of Islam and its adherents believe that [Ali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali "Ali") succeeded Muhammad and further places emphasis on Muhammad's family.
- There are also Muslim revivalist movements such as [Muwahhidism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muwahhidism "Muwahhidism") and [Salafism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salafism "Salafism").

Other denominations of Islam include [Nation of Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nation_of_Islam "Nation of Islam"), [Ibadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibadi "Ibadi"), [Sufism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufism "Sufism"), [Quranism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quranism "Quranism"), [Mahdavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahdavia "Mahdavia"), [Ahmadiyya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmadiyya "Ahmadiyya") and [non-denominational Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-denominational_Muslims "Non-denominational Muslims"). [Wahhabism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wahhabism "Wahhabism") is the dominant Muslim [schools of thought](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maddhab "Maddhab") in the [Kingdom of Saudi Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Saudi_Arabia "Kingdom of Saudi Arabia").

#### Other

Judaism, Christianity, and Islam are the three most popular Abrahamic faiths, however there are smaller and newer traditions that lay claim to the designation of Abrahamic as well.[^151]

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/dc/Lotus_Temple_in_New_Delhi_03-2016.jpg/500px-Lotus_Temple_in_New_Delhi_03-2016.jpg)

The Baháʼí Lotus Temple in Delhi

For example, the [Baháʼí Faith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bah%C3%A1%CA%BC%C3%AD_Faith "Baháʼí Faith") is a [new religious movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_religious_movement "New religious movement") that has links to the major Abrahamic religions as well as other religions (e.g., of Eastern philosophy). Founded in 19th-century Iran, it teaches the unity of all religious philosophies [^152] and accepts all of the prophets of Judaism, Christianity, and Islam as well as additional prophets (Buddha, Mahavira), including its founder [Bahá'u'lláh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bah%C3%A1%27u%27ll%C3%A1h "Bahá'u'lláh"). It is an offshoot of [Bábism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B%C3%A1bism "Bábism"). One of its divisions is the [Orthodox Baháʼí Faith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthodox_Bah%C3%A1%CA%BC%C3%AD_Faith "Orthodox Baháʼí Faith").[^153]<sup><span title="Page / location: 48–49">: 48–49 </span></sup> 

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8d/PikiWiki_Israel_45144_Nabi_Shuayb.JPG/500px-PikiWiki_Israel_45144_Nabi_Shuayb.JPG)

The shrine of Nabi Shu'ayb complex is revered as the foremost religious site in the Druze religion

Even smaller regional Abrahamic groups also exist, including [Samaritanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samaritanism "Samaritanism") (primarily in Israel and the [State of Palestine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_of_Palestine "State of Palestine")), the [Rastafari movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rastafari_movement "Rastafari movement") (primarily in Jamaica), and [Druze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Druze "Druze") (primarily in [Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Druze_in_Syria "Druze in Syria"), [Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Druze_in_Lebanon "Druze in Lebanon"), and [Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Druze_in_Israel "Druze in Israel")).

The Druze faith originally developed out of [Isma'ilism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isma%27ilism "Isma'ilism"), and it has sometimes been considered an [Islamic school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_schools_and_branches "Islamic schools and branches") by some Islamic authorities, but Druze themselves do not identify as [Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslims "Muslims").[^154] [^155] [^156] [^157] Scholars classify the Druze faith as an independent Abrahamic religion because it developed its own unique doctrines and eventually separated from both Isma'ilism and Islam altogether.[^158] [^159] One of these doctrines includes the belief that [Al-Ḥākim bi-Amr Allāh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Hakim_bi-Amr_Allah "Al-Hakim bi-Amr Allah") was an [incarnation of God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incarnation "Incarnation").[^160]

[Mandaeism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandaeism "Mandaeism"), sometimes also known as Sabianism (after the mysterious [Sabians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabians "Sabians") mentioned in the Quran, a name historically claimed by several religious groups),[^161] is a [Gnostic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gnosticism "Gnosticism"), [monotheistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotheistic "Monotheistic") and [ethnic religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_religion "Ethnic religion").[^162]<sup><span title="Page / location: 4">: 4 </span></sup>  [^163]<sup><span title="Page / location: 1">: 1 </span></sup>  Its adherents, the [Mandaeans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandaeans "Mandaeans"), consider [John the Baptist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_the_Baptist "John the Baptist") to be their chief prophet.[^162] Mandaeans are the last surviving Gnostics from antiquity.[^164]

### Indian religions

[Indian religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_religions "Indian religions") are practiced or were founded in the [Indian subcontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_subcontinent "Indian subcontinent"). They are sometimes classified as the _dharmic religions_, as they all feature [dharma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharma "Dharma"), the specific law of reality and duties expected according to the religion.[^165]

#### Hinduism

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b0/Sree_Padmanabhaswamy_temple_Thiruvananthapuram%2C_kerala.jpg/500px-Sree_Padmanabhaswamy_temple_Thiruvananthapuram%2C_kerala.jpg)

The Padmanabhaswamy Temple is a significant temple of the Hindu god Vishnu in Thiruvananthapuram, India.

[Hinduism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism "Hinduism") is also called _Vaidika Dharma_, the _[dharma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharma "Dharma")_ of the [Vedas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedas "Vedas"),[^166] although many practitioners refer to their religion as _[Sanātana Dharma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San%C4%81tana_Dharma "Sanātana Dharma")_ ("the Eternal Dharma") which refers to the idea that its origins lie beyond [human history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_history "Human history"). _Vaidika Dharma_ is a [synecdoche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synecdoche "Synecdoche") describing the similar philosophies of [Vaishnavism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaishnavism "Vaishnavism"), [Shaivism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shaivism "Shaivism"), and [related groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_denominations "Hindu denominations") practiced or founded in the [Indian subcontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_subcontinent "Indian subcontinent"). Concepts most of them share in common include [karma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karma "Karma"), [caste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caste "Caste"), [reincarnation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reincarnation "Reincarnation"), [mantras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mantra "Mantra"), [yantras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yantra "Yantra"), and [darśana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dar%C5%9Bana "Darśana").[^2] Deities in Hinduism are referred to as [Deva](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deva_(Hinduism)> "Deva (Hinduism)") (masculine) and [Devi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devi "Devi") (feminine).[^167] [^168] [^169] Major deities include [Vishnu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vishnu "Vishnu"), [Lakshmi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakshmi "Lakshmi"), [Shiva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shiva "Shiva"), [Parvati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parvati "Parvati"), [Brahma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahma "Brahma") and [Saraswati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saraswati "Saraswati"). These deities have distinct and complex personalities yet are often viewed as aspects of the same Ultimate Reality called [Brahman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahman "Brahman").[^170] Hinduism is one of the most ancient of still-active religious belief systems,[^171] [^172] with origins perhaps as far back as prehistoric times.[^173] Therefore, Hinduism has been called the oldest religion in the world.

#### Jainism

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f2/Shravanabelagola_Bahubali_wideframe.jpg/500px-Shravanabelagola_Bahubali_wideframe.jpg)

The 10th century Gommateshwara statue in Karnataka

[Jainism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jainism "Jainism"), taught primarily by [Rishabhanatha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rishabhanatha "Rishabhanatha") (the founder of [ahimsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahimsa "Ahimsa")) is an ancient Indian religion that prescribes a path of [non-violence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-violence "Non-violence"), [truth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truth "Truth") and [anekantavada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anekantavada "Anekantavada") for all forms of living beings in this universe; which helps them to eliminate all the [Karmas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karma_in_Jainism "Karma in Jainism"), and hence to attain freedom from the cycle of birth and death ([saṃsāra](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sa%E1%B9%83s%C4%81ra_(Jainism)> "Saṃsāra (Jainism)")), that is, achieving [nirvana](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moksha_(Jainism)> "Moksha (Jainism)"). Jains are found mostly in India. According to Dundas, outside of the Jain tradition, historians date the [Mahavira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahavira "Mahavira") as about contemporaneous with the [Buddha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddha "Buddha") in the 5th-century BCE, and accordingly the historical [Parshvanatha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parshvanatha "Parshvanatha"), based on the c. 250-year gap, is placed in 8th or 7th century BCE.[^174]

- [Digambara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digambara "Digambara") Jainism (or sky-clad) is mainly practiced in South India. Their holy books are [Pravachanasara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pravachanasara "Pravachanasara") and [Samayasara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samayasara "Samayasara") written by their Prophets [Kundakunda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kundakunda "Kundakunda") and [Amritchandra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amritchandra "Amritchandra") as their [original canon](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jain_Agamas_(Digambara)> "Jain Agamas (Digambara)") is lost.
- [Shwetambara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shwetambara "Shwetambara") Jainism (or white-clad) is mainly practiced in Western India. Their holy books are [Jain Agamas](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jain_Agamas_(%C5%9Av%C4%93t%C4%81mbara)> "Jain Agamas (Śvētāmbara)"), written by their Prophet [Sthulibhadra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sthulibhadra "Sthulibhadra").

#### Buddhism

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/be/Open_front_door_over_Wat_Mixay_and_praying_bhikkhus%2C_Vientiane%2C_Laos.jpg/500px-Open_front_door_over_Wat_Mixay_and_praying_bhikkhus%2C_Vientiane%2C_Laos.jpg)

Wat Mixay Buddhist shrine in Vientiane, Laos

[Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism "Buddhism") was founded by [Siddhartha Gautama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gautama_Buddha "Gautama Buddha") in the 5th century BCE. Buddhists generally agree that Gotama aimed to help [sentient beings](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentient_beings_(Buddhism)> "Sentient beings (Buddhism)") end their [suffering (dukkha)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dukkha "Dukkha") by understanding the [true nature of phenomena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharma "Dharma"), thereby escaping the cycle of suffering and rebirth ([saṃsāra](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sa%E1%B9%83s%C4%81ra_(Buddhism)> "Saṃsāra (Buddhism)")), that is, achieving [nirvana](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nirvana_(Buddhism)> "Nirvana (Buddhism)").

- [Theravada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theravada "Theravada") Buddhism, which is practiced mainly in [Sri Lanka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sri_Lanka "Sri Lanka") and Southeast Asia alongside folk religion, shares some characteristics of Indian religions. It is based in a large collection of texts called the [Pali Canon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pali_Canon "Pali Canon").
- [Mahayana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahayana "Mahayana") Buddhism (or the Great Vehicle) under which are a multitude of doctrines that became prominent [in China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism_in_China "Buddhism in China") and are still relevant [in Vietnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism_in_Vietnam "Buddhism in Vietnam"), [Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism_in_Korea "Buddhism in Korea"), [Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism_in_Japan "Buddhism in Japan") and to a lesser extent [in Europe and the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism_in_the_West "Buddhism in the West"). Mahayana Buddhism includes such disparate teachings as [Zen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zen "Zen") or [Pure Land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pure_Land_Buddhism "Pure Land Buddhism").
  ![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/aa/Buda_souvenir_over_a_shelf.jpg/500px-Buda_souvenir_over_a_shelf.jpg)

Buddha in a wood shelf in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil

- [Vajrayana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vajrayana "Vajrayana") Buddhism first appeared in India in the 3rd century CE.[^175] It is currently most prominent in the Himalaya regions [^176] and extends across all of Asia [^177] (cf. [Mikkyō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikky%C5%8D "Mikkyō")).
- Two notable new Buddhist sects are [Hòa Hảo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%C3%B2a_H%E1%BA%A3o "Hòa Hảo") and the [Navayana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navayana "Navayana") ([Dalit Buddhist movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dalit_Buddhist_movement "Dalit Buddhist movement")), which were developed separately in the 20th century.

#### Sikhism

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e1/Miniature_of_Guru_Nanak_from_Astronomical_treatise.jpg/500px-Miniature_of_Guru_Nanak_from_Astronomical_treatise.jpg)

[Sikhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikhism "Sikhism") is a [panentheistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panentheistic "Panentheistic") religion founded on the teachings of [Guru Nanak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guru_Nanak "Guru Nanak") and ten successive [Sikh gurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikh_gurus "Sikh gurus") in 15th-century [Punjab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punjab_region "Punjab region"). It is the [fifth-largest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_religious_groups "Major religious groups") [organized religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organized_religion "Organized religion") in the world, with approximately 30 million Sikhs.[^178] [^179] [Sikhs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikh "Sikh") are expected to embody the qualities of a _Sant-Sipāhī_ —a saint-soldier, have control over one's internal [vices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_Thieves "Five Thieves") and be able to be constantly immersed in virtues clarified in the [Guru Granth Sahib](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guru_Granth_Sahib "Guru Granth Sahib"). The principal beliefs of Sikhi are faith in _[Waheguru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waheguru "Waheguru")_ —represented by the phrase _[ik ōaṅkār](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ik_Onkar "Ik Onkar")_, one cosmic divine actioner (God), who prevails in everything, along with a [praxis](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Praxis_(process)> "Praxis (process)") in which the Sikh is enjoined to engage in social reform through the pursuit of justice for all human beings.

[Indigenous religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_religions "Indigenous religions") or [folk religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_religion "Ethnic religion") refers to a broad category of traditional religions that can be characterised by [shamanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shamanism "Shamanism"), [animism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animism "Animism") and [ancestor worship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancestor_worship "Ancestor worship"), where traditional means "indigenous, that which is aboriginal or foundational, handed down from generation to generation...".[^180] These are religions that are closely associated with a particular group of people, ethnicity or tribe; they often have no formal creeds or sacred texts.[^181] Some religions are [syncretic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syncretic "Syncretic"), fusing diverse religious beliefs and practices.[^182]

Folk religions are often omitted as a category in surveys even in countries where they are widely practiced, e.g., in China.[^181]

### Traditional African

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/24/Brooklyn_Museum_1992.133.4_Figure_of_Shango_on_Horseback.jpg/500px-Brooklyn_Museum_1992.133.4_Figure_of_Shango_on_Horseback.jpg)

[Iranian religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_religions "Iranian religions") are ancient religions whose roots predate the [Islamization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamization "Islamization") of [Greater Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Iran "Greater Iran"). ### Law

The study of law and religion is a relatively new field, with several thousand scholars involved in law schools, and academic departments including political science, religion, and history since 1980.[^204] Scholars in the field are not only focused on strictly legal issues about religious freedom or non-establishment, but also study religions as they are qualified through judicial discourses or legal understanding of religious phenomena. Exponents look at canon law, natural law, and state law, often in a comparative perspective.[^205] [^206] Specialists have explored themes in Western history regarding Christianity and justice and mercy, rule and equity, and discipline and love.[^207] Common topics of interest include marriage and the family [^208] and human rights.[^209] Outside of Christianity, scholars have looked at law and religion links in the Muslim Middle East [^210] and pagan Rome.[^211]

Studies have focused on [secularization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secularization "Secularization").[^212] [^213] In particular, the issue of wearing religious symbols in public, such as headscarves that are banned in French schools, have received scholarly attention in the context of human rights and feminism.[^214]

### Science

[Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science "Science") acknowledges reason and [empirical evidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empirical_evidence "Empirical evidence"); and religions include [revelation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revelation "Revelation"), [faith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faith "Faith") and [sacredness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacredness "Sacredness") whilst also acknowledging philosophical and [metaphysical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysical "Metaphysical") explanations with regard to the study of the universe. Both science and religion are not monolithic, timeless, or static because both are complex social and cultural endeavors that have changed through time across languages and cultures.[^215]

The concepts of science and religion are a recent invention: the term religion emerged in the 17th century in the midst of colonization and globalization and the Protestant Reformation.[^5] [^25] The term science emerged in the 19th century out of [natural philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_philosophy "Natural philosophy") in the midst of attempts to narrowly define those who studied nature ([natural science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_science "Natural science")),[^25] [^216] [^217] and the phrase religion and science emerged in the 19th century due to the reification of both concepts.[^25] It was in the 19th century that the terms Buddhism, Hinduism, Taoism, and Confucianism first emerged.[^25] In the ancient and medieval world, the etymological Latin roots of both science (_scientia_) and religion (_religio_) were understood as inner qualities of the individual or virtues, never as doctrines, practices, or actual sources of knowledge.[^25]

In general, the [scientific method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_method "Scientific method") gains knowledge by testing hypotheses to develop [theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theories "Theories") through elucidation of facts or evaluation by [experiments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experiment "Experiment") and thus only answers [cosmological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_cosmology "Physical cosmology") questions about the [universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universe "Universe") that can be observed and measured. It develops [theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory "Theory") of the world which best fit physically observed evidence. All scientific knowledge is subject to later refinement, or even rejection, in the face of additional evidence. Scientific theories that have an overwhelming preponderance of favorable evidence are often treated as _[de facto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_facto "De facto")_ verities in general parlance, such as the theories of [general relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_relativity "General relativity") and [natural selection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_selection "Natural selection") to explain respectively the mechanisms of [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity "Gravity") and [evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution "Evolution").

Religion does not have a method per se partly because religions emerge through time from diverse cultures and it is an attempt to find meaning in the world, and to explain humanity's place in it and relationship to it and to any posited entities. In terms of Christian theology and ultimate truths, people rely on reason, experience, scripture, and tradition to test and gauge what they experience and what they should believe. Furthermore, religious models, understanding, and metaphors are also revisable, as are scientific models.[^218]

Regarding religion and science, [Albert Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein "Albert Einstein") states (1940): "For science can only ascertain what is, but not what should be, and outside of its domain value judgments of all kinds remain necessary.[^219] Religion, on the other hand, deals only with evaluations of human thought and action; it cannot justifiably speak of facts and relationships between facts [^219]...Now, even though the realms of religion and science in themselves are clearly marked off from each other, nevertheless there exist between the two strong reciprocal relationships and dependencies. Though religion may be that which determine the goals, it has, nevertheless, learned from science, in the broadest sense, what means will contribute to the attainment of the goals it has set up." [^220]

### Morality

Many religions have value frameworks regarding personal behavior meant to guide adherents in determining between right and wrong. These include the [Five Vows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_Vows "Five Vows") of Jainism, Judaism's [halakha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halakha "Halakha"), Islam's [sharia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharia "Sharia"), Catholicism's [canon law](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canon_law_(Catholic_Church)> "Canon law (Catholic Church)"), Buddhism's [Noble Eightfold Path](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path "Noble Eightfold Path"), and Zoroastrianism's [good thoughts, good words, and good deeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theological_Aspects_of_the_Avesta "Theological Aspects of the Avesta") concept, among others.[^221]

Religion and morality are not synonymous. While it is often assumed in Christian thought that morality is ultimately based in religion, it can also have a [secular basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secular_morality "Secular morality").[^222]

The study of religion and morality can be contentious due to ethnocentric views on morality, failure to distinguish between in group and out group altruism, and inconsistent definitions of religiosity.

### Politics

#### Impact

Religion has had a significant impact on the political system in many countries.[^223] Notably, most Muslim-majority countries adopt various aspects of [sharia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharia "Sharia"), the Islamic law.[^224] Some countries even define themselves in religious terms, such as [The Islamic Republic of Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran "Iran"). The sharia thus affects up to 23% of the global population, or 1.57 billion people who are [Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_world "Muslim world"). However, religion also affects political decisions in many western countries. For instance, in the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States "United States"), 51% of voters would be less likely to vote for a presidential candidate who did not believe in God, and only 6% more likely.[^225] Christians made up 92% of members of the US Congress, compared with 71% of the general public (in 2014). At the same time, while 23% of US adults are religiously unaffiliated, only one former member of Congress ([Kyrsten Sinema](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyrsten_Sinema "Kyrsten Sinema"), Arizona), or 0.2% of that body, claims no religious affiliation.[^226] In most European countries, however, religion has a much smaller influence on politics [^227] although it used to be much more important. For instance, [same-sex marriage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Same-sex_marriage "Same-sex marriage") and [abortion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abortion "Abortion") were illegal in many European countries until recently, following Christian (usually [Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholicism "Catholicism")) doctrine. Several [European leaders are atheists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_atheists_in_politics_and_law "List of atheists in politics and law") (e.g., [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France "France") 's former president [Francois Hollande](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois_Hollande "François Hollande") or Greece's prime minister [Alexis Tsipras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexis_Tsipras "Alexis Tsipras")). In Asia, the role of religion differs widely between countries. For instance, [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India "India") is still one of the most religious countries and religion still has a strong impact on politics, given that Hindu nationalists have been targeting minorities like the Muslims and the Christians, who historically belonged to the lower castes.[^228] By contrast, countries such as [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_China "Religion in China") or [Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_Japan "Religion in Japan") are largely secular and thus religion has a much smaller impact on politics.

#### Secularism

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/d6/Ranjitsingh.gif)

Ranjit Singh established secular rule over Punjab in the early 19th century.

Secularization is the transformation of the politics of a society from close identification with a particular religion's values and institutions toward nonreligious values and [secular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secular "Secular") institutions. The purpose of this is frequently modernization or protection of the population's religious diversity.

### Economics

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/17/Religion_economy.png/600px-Religion_economy.png)

Average income correlates negatively with (self-defined) religiosity. 132

One study has found there is a negative correlation between self-defined religiosity and the wealth of nations.[^229] In other words, the richer a nation is, the less likely its inhabitants to call themselves religious, whatever this word means to them (Many people identify themselves as part of a religion (not irreligion) but do not self-identify as religious).[^229]

Sociologist and political economist [Max Weber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Weber "Max Weber") has argued that Protestant Christian countries are wealthier because of their [Protestant work ethic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant_work_ethic "Protestant work ethic").[^230] According to a study from 2015, [Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christians "Christians") hold the largest amount of wealth (55% of the total world wealth), followed by [Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslims "Muslims") (5.8%), [Hindus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindus "Hindus") (3.3%) and [Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jews "Jews") (1.1%). According to the same study it was found that adherents under the classification [Irreligion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irreligion "Irreligion") or other religions hold about 34.8% of the total global wealth (while making up only about 20% of the world population, see section on classification).[^231]

### Health

[Mayo Clinic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayo_Clinic "Mayo Clinic") researchers examined the association between religious involvement and spirituality, and physical health, mental health, health-related quality of life, and other health outcomes.[^232] The authors reported that: "Most studies have shown that religious involvement and spirituality are associated with better health outcomes, including greater longevity, coping skills, and health-related quality of life (even during terminal illness) and less anxiety, depression, and suicide." [^233]

The authors of a subsequent study concluded that the influence of religion on health is largely beneficial, based on a review of related literature.[^234] According to academic James W. Jones, several studies have discovered "positive correlations between religious belief and practice and mental and physical health and longevity." [^235]

An analysis of data from the 1998 US General Social Survey, whilst broadly confirming that religious activity was associated with better health and well-being, also suggested that the role of different dimensions of spirituality/religiosity in health is rather more complicated. The results suggested "that it may not be appropriate to generalize findings about the relationship between spirituality/religiosity and health from one form of spirituality/religiosity to another, across denominations, or to assume effects are uniform for men and women.[^236]

### Violence

Critics such as [Hector Avalos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hector_Avalos "Hector Avalos"),[^237] [Regina Schwartz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regina_Schwartz "Regina Schwartz"),[^238] [Christopher Hitchens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Hitchens "Christopher Hitchens"),[^239] and [Richard Dawkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Dawkins "Richard Dawkins") [^240] have argued that religions are inherently violent and harmful to society by using violence to promote their goals, in ways that are endorsed and exploited by their leaders.

Anthropologist Jack David Eller asserts that religion is not inherently violent, arguing "religion and violence are clearly compatible, but they are not identical." He asserts that "violence is neither essential to nor exclusive to religion" and that "virtually every form of religious violence has its nonreligious corollary." [^241] [^242]

#### Animal sacrifice

Some (but not all) religions practise [animal sacrifice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_sacrifice "Animal sacrifice"), the [ritual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ritual "Ritual") killing and offering of an animal to appease or maintain favour with a [deity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deity "Deity"). It has been banned in [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India "India").[^243]

### Superstition

Greek and Roman pagans, who saw their relations with the gods in political and social terms, scorned the man who constantly trembled with fear at the thought of the gods (_deisidaimonia_), as a slave might fear a cruel and capricious master. The Romans called such fear of the gods _superstitio_.[^244] Ancient Greek historian [Polybius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polybius "Polybius") described superstition in [ancient Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome "Ancient Rome") as an _[instrumentum regni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instrumentum_regni "Instrumentum regni")_, an instrument of maintaining the cohesion of the [Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire "Roman Empire").[^245]

Superstition has been described as the non-rational establishment of cause and effect.[^246] Religion is more complex and is often composed of social institutions and has a moral aspect. Some religions may include superstitions or make use of magical thinking. Adherents of one religion sometimes think of other religions as superstition.[^247] [^248] Some [atheists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheists "Atheists"), [deists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deists "Deists"), and [skeptics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skeptics "Skeptics") regard religious belief as superstition.

The Roman Catholic Church considers superstition to be sinful in the sense that it denotes a lack of trust in the divine providence of God and, as such, is a violation of the first of the Ten Commandments. The [Catechism of the Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catechism_of_the_Catholic_Church "Catechism of the Catholic Church") states that superstition "in some sense represents a perverse excess of religion" (para. #2110). "Superstition", it says, "is a deviation of religious feeling and of the practices this feeling imposes. It can even affect the worship we offer the true God, e.g., when one attributes an importance in some way magical to certain practices otherwise lawful or necessary. To attribute the efficacy of prayers or of sacramental signs to their mere external performance, apart from the interior dispositions that they demand is to fall into superstition. Cf. Matthew 23:16–22" (para. #2111)

The terms [atheist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheist "Atheist") (lack of belief in gods) and agnostic (belief in the unknowability of the existence of gods), though specifically contrary to theistic (e.g., Christian, Jewish, and Muslim) religious teachings, do not by definition mean the opposite of religious. The true opposite of religious is the word irreligious. [Irreligion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irreligion "Irreligion") describes an absence of any religion; [antireligion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antireligion "Antireligion") describes an active opposition or aversion toward religions in general. There are religions (including Buddhism and Taoism) that classify some of their followers as agnostic, atheistic, or [nontheistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nontheism "Nontheism"). For example, in ancient India, there were large atheistic movements and traditions ([Nirīśvaravāda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_atheism "Hindu atheism")) that rejected the [Vedas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedas "Vedas"), such as the atheistic [Ājīvika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%80j%C4%ABvika "Ājīvika") and the [Ajñana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aj%C3%B1ana "Ajñana") which taught agnosticism.

### Interfaith cooperation

Because religion continues to be recognized in Western thought as a universal impulse,[^249] many religious practitioners [^250] have aimed to band together in [interfaith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interfaith_dialogue "Interfaith dialogue") dialogue, cooperation, and [religious peacebuilding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_and_peacebuilding "Religion and peacebuilding"). The first major dialogue was the [Parliament of the World's Religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliament_of_the_World%27s_Religions "Parliament of the World's Religions") at the 1893 [Chicago World's Fair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World%27s_Columbian_Exposition "World's Columbian Exposition"), which affirmed universal values and recognition of the diversity of practices among different cultures.[^251] The 20th century has been especially fruitful in use of interfaith dialogue as a means of solving ethnic, political, or even religious conflict, with [Christian–Jewish reconciliation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian%E2%80%93Jewish_reconciliation "Christian–Jewish reconciliation") representing a complete reverse in the attitudes of many Christian communities towards Jews.[^252]

Recent interfaith initiatives include A Common Word, launched in 2007 and focused on bringing Muslim and Christian leaders together,[^253] the "C1 World Dialogue",[^254] the Common Ground initiative between Islam and Buddhism,[^255] and a [United Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations "United Nations") sponsored "World Interfaith Harmony Week".[^256] [^257]

### Culture

Culture and religion have usually been seen as closely related.[^52] [Paul Tillich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Tillich "Paul Tillich") looked at religion as the soul of culture and culture as the form or framework of religion.[^258] In his own words:

> Religion as ultimate concern is the meaning-giving substance of culture, and culture is the totality of forms in which the basic concern of religion expresses itself. In abbreviation: religion is the substance of culture, culture is the form of religion. Such a consideration definitely prevents the establishment of a dualism of religion and culture. Every religious act, not only in organized religion, but also in the most intimate movement of the soul, is culturally formed.[^259]

[Ernst Troeltsch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Troeltsch "Ernst Troeltsch"), similarly, looked at culture as the soil of religion and thought that, therefore, transplanting a religion from its original culture to a foreign culture would kill it in the same manner that transplanting a plant from its natural soil to an alien soil would kill it.[^260] However, there have been many attempts in the modern pluralistic situation to distinguish culture from religion.[^261] Domenic Marbaniang has argued that elements grounded on beliefs of a metaphysical nature (religious) are distinct from elements grounded on nature and the natural (cultural). For instance, language (with its grammar) is a cultural element while sacralization of language in which a particular religious scripture is written is more often a religious practice. The same applies to music and the arts.[^262]

## Criticism

Criticism of religion is [criticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criticism "Criticism") of the ideas, the truth, or the practice of religion, including its political and social implications.[^263]
