The **Brahmanas** ([/ ˈ b r ɑː m ə n ə z /](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"); [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit "Sanskrit"): ब्राह्मणम्, [IAST](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Alphabet_of_Sanskrit_Transliteration "International Alphabet of Sanskrit Transliteration"): *Brāhmaṇam*) are [Vedic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedas "Vedas") [śruti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%9Aruti "Śruti") works attached to the [Samhitas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samhita "Samhita") (hymns and mantras) of the [Rig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigveda "Rigveda"), [Sama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samaveda "Samaveda"), [Yajur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yajurveda "Yajurveda"), and [Atharva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atharvaveda "Atharvaveda") Vedas. They are a secondary layer or classification of [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit "Sanskrit") texts embedded within each Veda, which explain and instruct on the performance of [Vedic rituals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yajna "Yajna") (in which the related Samhitas are recited). In addition to explaining the symbolism and meaning of the [Samhitas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samhita "Samhita"), Brahmana literature also expounds scientific knowledge of the [Vedic Period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedic_period "Vedic period"), including [observational astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observational_astronomy "Observational astronomy") and, particularly in relation to altar construction, [geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry "Geometry"). Divergent in nature, some Brahmanas also contain mystical and philosophical material that constitutes [Aranyakas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aranyaka "Aranyaka") and [Upanishads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upanishad "Upanishad").[^1]

Each Veda has one or more of its own Brahmanas, and each Brahmana is generally associated with a particular [Shakha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakha "Shakha") or Vedic school. Less than twenty Brahmanas are currently extant, as most have been lost or destroyed. Dating of the final codification of the *Brahmanas* and associated Vedic texts is controversial, as they were likely recorded after several centuries of oral transmission.[^2] The oldest Brahmana is dated to about 900 [BCE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BCE "BCE"), while the most recent are dated to around 700 BCE.[^3] [^4]

*Brahmana* (or *Brāhmaṇam*, [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit "Sanskrit"): ब्राह्मणम्) can be loosely translated as ' [explanations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explanation "Explanation") of sacred knowledge or [doctrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctrine "Doctrine") ' or ' [Brahmanical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmin "Brahmin") explanation'.[^5] According to the [Monier-Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monier_Monier-Williams "Monier Monier-Williams") Sanskrit dictionary, 'Brahmana' means:[^6]

- 'Explanations of sacred knowledge or doctrine \[especially\] for the use of the [Brāhmans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmin "Brahmin") in their [sacrifices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yajna "Yajna") '.
- 'The Brāhmaṇa portion of the [Veda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedas "Vedas") (as distinct from its [Mantra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samhita "Samhita") and [Upanishad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upanishads "Upanishads") portion) and consisting of a class of works called Brāhmaṇas (they contain rules for the employment of the Mantras or hymns at various sacrifices, with detailed explanations of their origin and meaning and numerous old legends)'.
- According to ' [Sāyaṇa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayana "Sayana") to contain two parts: 1. *vidhi*, rules or directions for \[sacrificial\] rites \[and\] 2. *artha-vāda*, explanatory remarks'.

### Etymology

[M. Haug](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Haug "Martin Haug") states that [etymologically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etymology "Etymology"), 'the word \['Brahmana' or 'Brahmanam'\] is derived from [brahman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmin "Brahmin") which properly signifies the [Brahma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahman "Brahman") priest who must know all [Vedas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedas "Vedas"), and understand the whole course and meaning of the [sacrifice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yajna "Yajna")... the [dictum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dictum "Dictum") of such a Brahma priest who passed as a great authority, was called a Brahmanam'.[^7]

### Synonyms

S. Shrava states that [synonyms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synonym "Synonym") of the word 'Brahmana' include:[^8]

- **Vyakhyana:** In Brahmana works such as the [Shatapatha Brahmana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shatapatha_Brahmana "Shatapatha Brahmana") (6.7.4.6),[^9] 'the word *vyakhyana* also is a [synonym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synonym "Synonym") of the commonly used word brahmana'. Definitions of 'Vyakhyana' (व्याख्यान) include 'explaining', 'exposition', 'elucidation', 'expounding', 'interpretation', and 'lecture'.[^10]
- **Pravachana':** Another 'synonym of the brahmana word is *pravachana* '. Definitions of 'Pravachana' (प्रवचन) include 'expounding', 'teaching', 'exposition', and 'oral instruction'.[^11]
- **Vijnayate:** Is 'used to denote a reference from a brahmana work. The word has first of all been used in the [Gopatha Brahmana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gopatha_Brahmana "Gopatha Brahmana") '. Definitions of 'Vijnayate' include 'It is a fact that...' and 'be distinguished or discerned'.[^12]

## Overview

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/91/Yajna1.jpg/500px-Yajna1.jpg)

Performance of Yajna.

R. Dalal states that the 'Brahmanas are texts attached to the [Samhitas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samhita "Samhita") \[hymns\] – [Rig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigveda "Rigveda"), [Sama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samaveda "Samaveda"), [Yajur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yajurveda "Yajurveda") and [Atharva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atharvaveda "Atharvaveda") Vedas – and provide explanations of these and guidance for the priests in [sacrificial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yajna "Yajna") rituals'.[^13] S. Shri elaborates, stating 'Brahmanas explain the hymns of the [Samhitas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samhita "Samhita") and are in both prose and verse form... The Brahmanas are divided into Vidhi and Arthavada. Vidhi are commands in the performance of Vedic [sacrifices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yajna "Yajna"), and Arthavada praises the rituals, the glory of the [Devas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deva_\(Hinduism\) "Deva (Hinduism)") and so on. The belief in [reincarnation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reincarnation "Reincarnation") and transmigration of soul started with \[the\] Brahmanas... \[The\] Brahmana period ends around 500 BC\[E\] with the emergence of [Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism "Buddhism") and it overlaps the period of [Aranyakas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aranyaka "Aranyaka"), [Sutras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sutra "Sutra"), [Smritis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smriti "Smriti") and the first [Upanishads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upanishads "Upanishads") '.[^14]

M. Haug states that the 'Veda, or scripture of the Brahmans, consists, according to the opinion of the most eminent divines of [Hindustan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindustan "Hindustan"), of two principal parts, viz. Mantra \[[Samhita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samhita "Samhita")\] and Brahmanam... Each of the four Vedas ([Rik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigveda "Rigveda"), [Yajus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yajurveda "Yajurveda"), [Saman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samaveda "Samaveda"), and [Atharvan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atharvaveda "Atharvaveda")) has a Mantra, as well as a Brahmana portion. The difference between both may be briefly stated as follows: That part which contains the sacred prayers, the invocations of the different deities, the sacred verses for chanting at the [sacrifices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yajna "Yajna"), the sacrificial formulas \[is\] called *Mantra*... The Brahmanam \[part\] always presupposes the Mantra; for without the latter it would have no meaning... \[they contain\] speculations on the meaning of the mantras, gives precepts for their application, relates stories of their origin... and explains the secret meaning of the latter'.[^7]

[J. Eggeling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Eggeling "Julius Eggeling") states that 'While the Brâhma *n* as are thus our oldest sources from which a comprehensive view of the [sacrificial ceremonial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yajna "Yajna") can be obtained, they also throw a great deal of light on the earliest [metaphysical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics "Metaphysics") and [linguistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistics "Linguistics") speculations of the [Hindus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindus "Hindus"). Another, even more interesting feature of these works, consists in the numerous [legends](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legend "Legend") scattered through them. From the archaic style in which these [mythological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myth "Myth") tales are generally composed, as well as from the fact that not a few of them are found in Brâhma *n* as of different [schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakha "Shakha") and [Vedas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedas "Vedas"), though often with considerable variations, it is pretty evident that the ground-work of many of them goes back to times preceding the composition of the Brâhma *n* as'.[^15]

The [Indira Gandhi National Centre for the Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indira_Gandhi_National_Centre_for_the_Arts "Indira Gandhi National Centre for the Arts") (IGNCA) states that while 'the Upanishads speculate on the nature of the universe, and the relationship of the one and the many, the immanent and transcendental, the Brahmanas make concrete the world-view and the concepts through a highly developed system of ritual-yajna. This functions as a strategy for a continuous reminder of the inter-relatedness of man and nature, the five elements and the sources of energy'.[^16]

The Brahmanas are particularly noted for their instructions on the proper performance of rituals, as well as explanations on the symbolic importance of sacred words and ritual actions.[^17] Academics such as P. Alper, [K. Klostermaier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klaus_Klostermaier "Klaus Klostermaier") and [F.M, Muller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Muller "Max Muller") state that these instructions insist on exact pronunciation (accent),[^18] *[chhandas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedic_meter "Vedic meter")* (छन्दः, meters), precise pitch, with coordinated movement of hand and fingers – that is, perfect delivery.[^19] [^20] Klostermaier adds that the [Satapatha Brahamana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shatapatha_Brahmana "Shatapatha Brahmana"), for example, states that verbal perfection made a mantra infallible, while one mistake made it powerless.[^19] Scholars suggest that this [orthological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthology_\(language\) "Orthology (language)") perfection preserved Vedas in an age when writing technology was not in vogue, and the voluminous collection of Vedic knowledge were taught to and memorized by dedicated students through [Svādhyāya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sv%C4%81dhy%C4%81ya#Svadhyaya_as_a_historical_practice "Svādhyāya"), then remembered and verbally transmitted from one generation to the next.[^19] [^21] It seems breaking silence too early in at least one ritual is permissible in the Satapatha (1.1.4.9), where 'in that case mutter some Rik \[[Rigveda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigveda "Rigveda")\] or Yagus-text \[[Yajurveda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yajurveda "Yajurveda")\] addressed to [Vishnu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vishnu "Vishnu"); for Vishnu is the sacrifice, so that he thereby regains obtains a hold on the [sacrifice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yajna "Yajna"), and penance is there by done by him'.[^22]

The Adbhuta Brahmana is from the last part of the Sadvimsa Brahmana and deals with 'omens and supernatural things'.<sup><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#fn:29">29</a></sup> Attributed by Caland to the Kuthuma-Ranayaniya Shakha,<sup><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#fn:30">30</a></sup> but by Macdonell to the Tandin <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakha">Shakha</a>.<sup><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#fn:31">31</a></sup></p><p><b><sup>d:</sup></b> Also called the Devatadhyaya Brahmana.</p><p><b><sup>e:</sup></b> The Mantra Brahmana (also called the Samaveda-Mantrabrahmana, SMB) is from the first two chapters of the Chandogya Brahmana (also called the Chandogyaopanishad and the Upanishad Brahmana); the remaining chapters of the Chandogya Brahmana form the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chandogya_Upanishad">Chandogya Upanishad</a>.</p><p><b><sup>f:</sup></b> Also called the Catapatha Brahmana (CB; this abbreviation also denotes the Mâdhyandina <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recension">recension</a>)</p><p><b><sup>g:</sup></b> Part of the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taittiriya_Shakha">Taittiriya</a> <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aranyaka">Aranyaka</a>; explains the Pravargya rite. Generally not considered a Brahmana-proper, although it has been published as one.<sup><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#fn:32">32</a></sup></p><p><b><sup>h:</sup></b> Linked with the Krishna (Black) Yajurveda, it is 'actually part of the Vadhula Shrauta Sutra'.<sup><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#fn:13">13</a></sup></p></td></tr></tbody></table>

S. Sharva states that in 'the brahmana literature this word \['brahmana'\] has been commonly used as detailing the ritualism related to the different sacrifices or [yajnas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yajna "Yajna")... The known [recensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recension "Recension") \[i.e. schools or [Shakhas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakha "Shakha")\] of the [Vedas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedas "Vedas"), all had separate brahmanas. Most of these brahmanas are [not extant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_literary_work "Lost literary work").... \[[Panini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C4%81%E1%B9%87ini "Pāṇini")\] differentiates between the old and the new brahmanas... \[he asked\] Was it when [Krishna Dvaipayana Vyasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vyasa "Vyasa") had propounded the Vedic recensions? The brahmanas which had been propounded prior to the exposition of recensions by \[[Vyasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vyasa "Vyasa")\] were called as old brahmanas and those which had been expounded by his disciples were known as new brahmanas'.[^8]

## Rigveda

The [Aitareya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aitareya_Brahmana "Aitareya Brahmana"), Kausitaki, and [Samkhyana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sankhyayana_Brahmana "Sankhyayana Brahmana") Brahmanas are the two (or three) known extant Brahmanas of the [Rigveda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigveda "Rigveda"). [A.B. Keith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Berriedale_Keith "Arthur Berriedale Keith"), a translator of the Aitareya and Kausitaki Brahmanas, states that it is 'almost certainly the case that these two [\[Kausitaki and Samkhyana\] Brahmanas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sankhyayana_Brahmana "Sankhyayana Brahmana") represent for us the development of a single tradition, and that there must have been a time when there existed a single... text \[from which they were developed and diverged\]'.[^33] Although S. Shrava considers the Kausitaki and Samkhyana Brahmanas to be separate although very similar works,[^8] M. Haug considers them to be the same work referred to by different names.[^7]

### Aitareya Brahmana

> The [sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun "Sun") does never set nor rise. When people think the sun is setting (it is not so). For, after having arrived at the end of the day, it makes itself produce two opposite effects, making night to what is below and day to what is on the other side...Having reached the end of the night, it makes itself produce two opposite effects, making day to what is below and night to what is on the other side. In fact, the sun never sets. Nor does it set for him who has such a knowledge. Such a one becomes united with the sun, assumes its form, and enters its place.

— Aitareya Brahmanam of the RigVeda, translated by Martin Haug (1922), Book 3, Chapter 4, Section 44 [^34]

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/98/1827_CE_manuscript_copy%2C_Vedic_era_Aitareya_Brahmana%2C_Schoyen_Collection_Norway.jpg/500px-1827_CE_manuscript_copy%2C_Vedic_era_Aitareya_Brahmana%2C_Schoyen_Collection_Norway.jpg)

Page from the Aitareya Brahmana.

As detailed in the main article, the [Aitareya Brahmana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aitareya_Brahmana "Aitareya Brahmana") (AB) is ascribed to the sage Mahidasa Aitareya of the [Shakala Shakha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakala_Shakha "Shakala Shakha") (Shakala school) of the [Rigveda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigveda "Rigveda"), and is estimated to have been recorded around 600-400 [BCE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Era "Common Era").[^33] It is also linked with the Ashvalayana Shakha.[^13] The text itself consists of eight *pañcikā* s (books), each containing five *adhyaya* s (chapters), totaling forty in all. C. Majumdar states that 'it deals principally with the great [Soma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soma_\(drink\) "Soma (drink)") sacrifices and the different ceremonies of royal inauguration'.[^29]

Haug states that the legend about this Brahmana, as told by [Sayana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayana "Sayana"), is that the 'name "Aitareya" is by Indian tradition traced to *Itara*... An ancient [Risi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rishi "Rishi") had among his many wives one who was called *Itara*. She had a son *Mahidasa* by name \[i.e. Mahidasa Aitareya\]... The Risi preferred the sons of his other wives to Mahidasa, and went even so far as to insult him once by placing all his other children in his lap to his exclusion. His mother, grieved at this ill-treatment of her son, prayed to her family deity (*[Kuladevata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuladevata "Kuladevata")*), \[and\] the Earth ([Bhumi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bh%C5%ABmi "Bhūmi")), who appeared in her celestial form in the midst of the assembly, placed him on a throne (*[simhasana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simhasana "Simhasana")*), and gave him as a token of honour for his surpassing all other children in learning a boon (vara) which had the appearance of a Brahmana \[i.e. the Aitareya\]'.[^7] P. Deussen agrees, relating the same story.[^35] Notably, The story itself is remarkably similar to the legend of a [Vaishnava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaishnavism "Vaishnavism") boy called [Dhruva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhruva "Dhruva") in the [Puranas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puranas "Puranas") (e.g. [Bhagavata Purana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhagavata_Purana "Bhagavata Purana"), Canto 4, Chapter 8-12).[^36]

> The [gods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deva_\(Hinduism\) "Deva (Hinduism)") and the [Asuras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asura "Asura") were in conflict over these worlds. From them [Agni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agni "Agni") departed, and entered the seasons. The gods, having been victorious and having slain the Asuras, sought for him; [Yama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yama_\(Hinduism\) "Yama (Hinduism)") and [Varuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varuna "Varuna") discerned him. Him (the gods) invited, him they instructed, to him they offered a boon. He chose this as a boon, '(Give) me the fore-offering and the after-offerings for my own, and the [ghee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghee "Ghee") of the waters and make of plants.' Therefore they say 'Agni's are the fore-offerings and the after-offerings; Agni's is the butter.' Then indeed did the gods prosper, the Asuras were defeated. He prospers himself, his foe is defeated, who knows thus.

— Rigveda Brahmanas: The Aitareya And Kausitaki Brahmanas Of The [Rigveda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigveda "Rigveda"), translated by [Arthur Berriedale Keith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Berriedale_Keith "Arthur Berriedale Keith") (1920), Kausitaki Brahmana, Adhyaya I, Verse 2 [^33]

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/40/Tools_used_for_Yajna.jpg/500px-Tools_used_for_Yajna.jpg)

Tools used for Yajna.

The [Indira Gandhi National Centre for the Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indira_Gandhi_National_Centre_for_the_Arts "Indira Gandhi National Centre for the Arts") (IGNCA) states that the 'Kaushitaki Brahmana \[is\] associated with Baskala [Shakha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakha "Shakha") of \[the\] [Rigveda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigveda "Rigveda") and \[is\] also called Sankhyayana Brahmana. It is divided into thirty chapters \[adhyayas\] and 226 Khanda\[s\]. The first six chapters dealing with food sacrifice and the remaining to [Soma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soma_\(drink\) "Soma (drink)") sacrifice. This work is ascribed to [Sankhyayana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sankhyayana "Sankhyayana") or Kaushitaki'.[^37] S. Shrava disagrees, stating that it 'was once considered that \[the\] Kaushitaki or Samkhayana was the name of the same brahmana... \[but the Samkhayana\] differs, though slightly, from the Kaushitaki Brahmana'.[^8] C. Majumdar states that it 'deals not only with the [Soma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soma_\(drink\) "Soma (drink)"), but also other sacrifices'.[^29]

Keith estimates that the Kaushîtaki-brâhmana was recorded around 600–400 BCE, adding that it is more 'scientific' and 'logical' than the Aitareya Brahmana, although much 'of the material of the Kausitaki, and especially the legends, has been taken over by the Brahmana from a source common to it and the Aitareya, but the whole has been worked up into a harmonious unity which presents no such irregularities as are found in the Aitareya. It is clearly a redaction of the tradition of the school made deliberately after the redaction of the Aitareya'.[^33]

[Max Müller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_M%C3%BCller "Max Müller") states that the [Kaushitaki Upanishad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaushitaki_Upanishad "Kaushitaki Upanishad") – also called the *Kaushitaki Brahmana Upanishad* (KBU) – 'does not form part of the Kaushîtaki-brâhmana in 30 adhyâyas which we possess, and we must therefore account for its name by admitting that the [Âranyaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aranyaka "Aranyaka"), of which it formed a portion, could be reckoned as part of the Brâhmana literature of the [Rig-veda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigveda "Rigveda") (see Aitareya-âranyaka, Introduction, p. xcii), and that hence the [Upanishad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upanishads "Upanishads") might be called the Upanishad of the Brâhmana of the Kaushîtakins'.[^38]

## Samaveda

W. Caland states that of the [Samaveda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samaveda "Samaveda"), three [Shakhas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakha "Shakha") (schools or branches) 'are to be distinguished; that of the Kauthumas, that of the Ranayaniyas, and that of the Jaiminiyas'.[^39]

> [Visnu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vishnu "Vishnu") is the [sacrifice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yajna "Yajna"); what here (on this day) is not brought about, that he brings about through Vishnu (who is) the sacrifice.

— Pancavimsa Brahmana, translated by W. Caland (1931), Prapathaka XIII (13), Adhyaya 5, Verse 5 [^40]

[Caland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willem_Caland "Willem Caland") states that the [Panchavimsha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panchavimsha_Brahmana "Panchavimsha Brahmana") / Tandya Brahmana of the Kauthuma [Shakha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakha "Shakha") consists of 25 prapathakas (books or chapters).[^39] C. Majumdar states that it 'is one of the oldest and most important of Brahmanas. It contains many old legends, and includes the *Vratyastoma*, a ceremony by which people of non-Aryan stock could be admitted into the Aryan family'.[^29]

#### Sadvimsa Brahmana

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1f/1636_CE_Samaveda%2C_Sadvimsha_Brahmana_%28Pa%C3%B1cavi%E1%B9%83%C5%9Babr%C4%81hma%E1%B9%87a_supplement%29%2C_Benares_Sanskrit_college%2C_Edward_Cowell_collection%2C_sample_i%2C_Sanskrit%2C_Devanagari.jpg/500px-thumbnail.jpg)

Sadvimsha Brahmana page.

The Sadvimsa Brahmana is also of the Kauthuma Shakha, and consists of 5 adhyayas (lessons or chapters). Caland states it is 'a kind of appendix to the \[Panchavimsha Brahmana\], reckoned as its 26th book \[or chapter\]... The text clearly intends to supplement the Pancavimsabrahmana, hence its desultory character. It treats of the [Subrahmanya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subrahmanya "Subrahmanya") formula, of the one-day-rites that are destined to injure (*abhicara*) and other matters. This brahmana, at least partly, is presupposed by the Arseyakalpa and the Sutrakaras'.[^39]

##### Adbhuta Brahmana

Caland states that the Adbhuta Brahmana, also of the Kauthuma Shakha, is the 'latest part \[i.e. 5th adhyaya of the Sadvimsa Brahmana\], that which treats of Omina and Portenta \[[Omens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omen "Omen") and [Divination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divination "Divination")\]'.[^39] Majumdar agrees.[^29]

### Samavidhana Brahmana

Caland states that the Samavidhana Brahmana of the Kauthuma Shakha is 'in 3 prapathakas \[books or chapters\]... its aim is to explain how by chanting various samans \[hymns of the [Samaveda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samaveda "Samaveda")\] [^41] some end may be attained. It is probably older than one of the oldest dharmasastras, that of Gautama'.[^39] M. S. Bhat states that it is not properly a Brāhmaṇa text, but belongs to the [Vidhāna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vidh%C4%81na "Vidhāna") literature.[^42]

### Daivata Brahmana

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2a/12th-century_Samaveda_samhita_and_brahmanam%2C_Aranyaganam_Prapathaka_1-6%2C_page_1_front%2C_Raghunath_temple_archives%2C_Jammu.jpg/500px-12th-century_Samaveda_samhita_and_brahmanam%2C_Aranyaganam_Prapathaka_1-6%2C_page_1_front%2C_Raghunath_temple_archives%2C_Jammu.jpg)

Page from the Samaveda samhita and brahmanam.

Caland states that the Daivata Brahmana of the Kauthuma Shakha is 'in 3 prapathakas \[books or chapters\]... It deals with the deities to which the samans are addressed'.[^39] Dalal adds that the 'first part of the Devatadhyaya is the most important as it provides rules to determine the deities to whom the samans are dedicated. Another section ascribes colours to different verses, probably as aids to memory or for meditation... \[It\] includes some very late passages such as references to the four yugas or ages'.[^13]

### Samhitopanishad Brahmana

Caland states that the Samhitopanishad Brahmana of the Kauthuma Shakha is 'in 5 khandas \[books\]... It treats of the effects of recitation, the relation of the saman \[hymns of the [Samaveda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samaveda "Samaveda")\] and the words on which it is chanted, the *daksinas* to be given to the religious teacher'.[^39] Dalal agrees, stating that it 'describes the nature of the chants and their effects, and how the riks or Rig Vedic verses were converted into samans. Thus it reveals some of the hidden aspects of the [Sama Veda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samaveda "Samaveda") '.[^13]

### Arsheya Brahmana

Caland states that the Arsheya Brahmana of the Kauthuma Shakha is ''in 3 prapathakas \[books or chapters\]... This quasi-brahmana is, on the whole, nothing more than an anukramanika, a mere list of the names of the samans \[hymns of the [Samaveda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samaveda "Samaveda")\] occurring in the first two ganas \[of the Kauthumas, i.e. the Gramegeya-gana / Veya-gana and the Aramyegeya-gana / Aranya-gana\]'.[^39] The nature of the ganas noted are discussed in the same text. As illustrated below, this Brahmana is virtually identical to the Jaiminiya Arsheya Brahmana of the Jaiminiya [Shakha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakha "Shakha").

### Vamsha Brahmana

Caland states that the Vamsha Brahmana of the Kauthuma Shakha is 'in 3 khandas \[books\]... it contains the lists of teachers of the Samaveda'.[^39] Notably, Dalal adds that of the 53 teachers listed, the 'earliest teacher, [Kashyapa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashyapa "Kashyapa"), is said to have received the teaching from the god, [Agni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agni "Agni") '.[^13]

### Jaiminiya Brahmana

> He should proceed thus: Having taken a water-pot or a water-jar he should go pouring it out from the garhapatya to the ahavaniya with the verse: "Here [Visnu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vishnu "Vishnu") strode". The *rc* \[RigVeda verse, e.g. 1.22.17\] is a divine purification, water is a divine purification. Whatever here is unappeased of the [sacrifice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yajna "Yajna") and whatever is impure, for all that, water forms the means of appeasing. So by water they appease it.

— Jaiminya Brahmana, translated by Hendrik Bodewitz (1973), Book 1, Section 52 ('Vehicles or animals pass between the fires') [^43] [^44]

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/91/16th-century_Jaiminiya_Mimamsa_Sutra_Bhasya%2C_Sanskrit%2C_Telugu_script%2C_Andhra_Pradesh.jpg/960px-16th-century_Jaiminiya_Mimamsa_Sutra_Bhasya%2C_Sanskrit%2C_Telugu_script%2C_Andhra_Pradesh.jpg)

Page form the Mimamsa sutra of Jaiminimi, who also recorded the Jaiminiya Brahmana and other works. 45

It seems that this Brahmana has not been fully translated to date, or at least a full translation has not been made available.

S. Shrava states that the Jaiminiya Brahmana of the Jaiminiya Shakha, also called the T *alavakara Brahmana*, 'is divided into 1348 khandas \[verses\]... Many of the sentences of this brahmana are similar to those found in Tamdya, Sadavimsam, [Satapatha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shatapatha_Brahmana "Shatapatha Brahmana") \[Brahmanas\] and \[the\] [Taittirya Samhita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taittiriya_Shakha "Taittiriya Shakha") \[Krishna/Black [Yajurveda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yajurveda "Yajurveda")\]. Many of the hymns are found for the first time in it. Their composition is different from that available in Vedic literature. Most of the subjects described in it are completely new and are not found in other bramanas like Tamdya, etc... In the beginning khandas, details of daily oblation to the sacrificial fire are described... This brahmana was compiled by Jaimini a famous preceptor of [Samaveda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samaveda "Samaveda") and the worthy disciple of [Krishna Dvaipayana Vedavyasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vyasa "Vyasa") and his disciple Talavakara'.[^8]

Dalal states that the Jaiminiya Arsheya Brahmana of the Jaiminiya Shakha 'is similar to the Arsheya Brahmana of the Kauthuma school but for the fact that the names of the [rishis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rishi "Rishi") in the two are different. Unlike the Kauthuma texts, this lists only one rishi per saman'.[^13]

As detailed in the main article, also called the *Talavakara Upanishad Brahmana* and *Jaiminiyopanishad Brahmana*, it is considered an [Aranyaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aranyaka "Aranyaka") – not a Brahmana – and forms part of the [Kena Upanishad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kena_Upanishad "Kena Upanishad").

### Chandogya Brahmana

> Now that light which shines above this heaven, higher than all, higher than everything, in the highest world, beyond which there are no other worlds, that is the same light which is within man.

— Chandogya Upanishad, edited by F.M. Muller (1879), Prapathaka 3 (Prapathaka 5 of the Chandogya Brahmana), Khanda 13, Verse 7 [^46]

The [Indira Gandhi National Centre for the Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indira_Gandhi_National_Centre_for_the_Arts "Indira Gandhi National Centre for the Arts") (IGNCA) states that the Chandogya Brahmana, also called the *chanddogyopanishad Brahmana,* 'is divided into ten prapathakas (chapters). Its first two prapathakas (chapters) form the Mantra Brahmana (MB) and each of them is divided into eight khandas (sections). Prapathakas 3–10 form the [Chandogya Upanishad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chandogya_Upanishad "Chandogya Upanishad") '.[^47] K.G. Witz states it is of the Kauthuma Shakha (see below).[^48]

#### Mantra Brahmana

> ad etad dhridayam tava tad astu hridayam mama,  
> Yad idam hridayam mama tad astu hridayam tava

—Mantra Brahmana, [transliteration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transliteration "Transliteration") and translation of Chapter 1, Sukta 3 [^49]

> That heart of thine shall be mine,  
> and this heart of mine shall be thine.

K. G. Witz states that the Mantra Brahmana is 'a text in two chapters which mostly give Vedic Mantras which should be used in rites such as for birth and marriage. The combined text \[with 8 chapters forming the [Chandogya Upanishad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chandogya_Upanishad "Chandogya Upanishad")\] is \[also\] called \[the\] Upanishad Brahmana and is one of the eight canonical Brahmanas of the Kauthumas. The fact that the Upanishad was combined with the Mantra Brahmana into a single text is significant. Just as everyone in society is blessed and made part of the overall divine societal, social and world order by the household rites in the Mantra Brahmana, so everyone can direct his life toward the Infinite Reality by the numerous upasanas and vidyas of the Chandogya Upanishad.' [^48]

R. Mitra is quoted as stating that of 'the two portions differ greatly, and judged by them they appear to be productions of very different ages, though both are evidently relics of pretty remote antiquity. Of the two chapters of the Khandogya-Brahmana \[Chandogya Brahmana, forming the Mantra Brahmana\], the first includes eight suktas \[hymns\] on the ceremony of marriage and the rites necessary to be observed at the birth of a child. The first Sukta is intended to be recited when offering an oblation to [Agni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agni "Agni") on the occasion of a marriage, and its object is to pray for prosperity \[on\] behalf of the married couple. The second prays for a long life, kind relatives, and a numerous progeny \[i.e. children\]. The third is the marriage pledge by which the \[couple\] bind themselves to each other. Its spirit may be guessed from a single verse. In talking of the unanimity with which they will dwell, the bridegroom addresses his bride, "That heart of thine shall be mine, and this heart of mine shall be thine" \[as quoted above\]'.[^50]

## Yajurveda

> tarhi videgho māthava āsa | sarasvatyāṃ sa tata eva prāṅdahannabhīyāyemām pṛthivīṃ taṃ gotamaśca rāhūgaṇo videghaśca māthavaḥ paścāddahantamanvīyatuḥ sa imāḥ sarvā nadīratidadāha sadānīretyuttarādgirernirghāvati tāṃ haiva nātidadāha tāṃ ha sma tām purā brāhmaṇā na tarantyanatidagdhāgninā vaiśvānareṇeti

—Satapatha Brahmnana, [transliteration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transliteration "Transliteration") of Kanda I, Adhyâya IV, Brâhmana I, Verse 14 [^51]

> Mâthava, the Videgha, was at that time on the (river) Sarasvatî. He ([Agni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agni "Agni")) thence went burning along this earth towards the east; and Gotama Râhûgana and the Videgha Mâthava followed after him as he was burning along. He burnt over (dried up) all these rivers. Now that (river), which is called 'Sadânîrâ,' flows from the northern (Himâlaya) mountain: that one he did not burn over. That one the Brâhmans did not cross in former times, thinking, 'it has not been burnt over by Agni Vaisvânara.'

—Satapatha Brahmana, [translation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translation "Translation") by [Julius Eggeling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Eggeling "Julius Eggeling") (1900), Kanda I, Adhyâya IV, Brâhmana I, Verse 14 [^52]

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/25/13th-century_Shatapatha_Brahmana_14th_Khanda_Prapathaka_3-4%2C_page_1r_and_1v%2C_Sanskrit%2C_Devanagari_script.jpg/500px-13th-century_Shatapatha_Brahmana_14th_Khanda_Prapathaka_3-4%2C_page_1r_and_1v%2C_Sanskrit%2C_Devanagari_script.jpg)

Extract from a 13th-century manuscript of the Shatapatha Brahmana (Khanda 14).

The 'final form' of the Satapatha Brahmana is estimated to have been recorded around 1000–800 BCE, although it refers to [astronomical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy "Astronomy") phenomena dated to 2100 BCE, and, as quoted above, historical events such as the drying up of the [Sarasvati river](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarasvati_River "Sarasvati River"), which is believed to have occurred around 1900 BCE.[^53] It provides [scientific](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science "Science") knowledge of [geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry "Geometry") and [observational astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observational_astronomy "Observational astronomy") from the [Vedic period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedic_period "Vedic period"), and is considered significant in the development of [Vaishnavism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaishnavism "Vaishnavism") as the possible origin of several [Puranic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puranas "Puranas") legends and [avatars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avatar "Avatar") of the [RigVedic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigveda "Rigveda") god [Vishnu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vishnu "Vishnu"), all of which ([Matsya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matsya "Matsya"), [Kurma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurma "Kurma"), [Varaha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varaha "Varaha"), [Narasimha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narasimha "Narasimha"), and [Vamana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vamana "Vamana")) are listed in the [Dashavatara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dashavatara "Dashavatara").

[M. Winternitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moriz_Winternitz "Moriz Winternitz") states that this Brahmana is 'the best known, the most extensive, and doubtless, also on account of its contents, the most important of all the Brahmanas'.[^1] Eggeling states that 'The Brâhma *n* a of the Vâ *g* asaneyins bears the name of *S* atapatha, that is, the Brâhma *n* a 'of a hundred paths,' because it consists of a hundred lectures (adhyâyas). Both the [Vâ *g* asaneyi-sa *m* hitâ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yajurveda "Yajurveda") \[Yajurveda\] and the [*S* atapatha-brâhma *n* a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shatapatha_Brahmana "Shatapatha Brahmana") have come down to us in two different [recensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recension "Recension"), those of the [Mâdhyandina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madhyandina_Shakha "Madhyandina Shakha") and the [Kâ *n* va schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanva_Shakha "Kanva Shakha") ':[^15]

- **Mâdhyandina recension:** Ascribed to [Yājñavalkya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yajnavalkya "Yajnavalkya") Vājasaneya, it consists of 14 khandas ('books') which can be divided into two major parts. The first nine khandas are commentaries of the corresponding [samhita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samhita "Samhita") of the Śukla (white) [Yajurveda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yajurveda "Yajurveda"). The final five khandas cover supplementary and ritualistically newer material; and the final book constitutes the [Bṛhad-Āraṇyaka Upaniṣad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brihadaranyaka "Brihadaranyaka").[^15]
- **Kanva recension:** Ascribed to [Samkara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adi_Shankara "Adi Shankara"), it consists of 17 khandas. The IGNCA adds that 'the division of Kandika \[verses\] is more rational in the [Kanva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanva "Kanva") text than in the other... The name 'Satapatha', as Eggeling has suggested, might have been based on the number of Adhyayas \[chapters\] in the Madhyandina which is exactly one hundred. But the Kanva recension, which has one hundred and four Adhyayas is also known by the same name. In Indian tradition words like 'sata' and 'sahasra', indicating numbers, do not always stand for exact numbers'.[^16]

> When the completion of [yajna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yajna "Yajna") does not happen in a year (*[samvatsara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samvatsara "Samvatsara")*) then everything is not stable. Then one has to seek the grace of [Vishnu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vishnu "Vishnu") ([Vamana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vamana "Vamana")) by performing a special rite on the *[ekadashi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ekadashi "Ekadashi")* day. Yajna means Vishnu (worshipping Vishnu). They perform yajna only for stabilising. They depend on [Indra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indra "Indra") and [Agni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agni "Agni"). Indra and Agni give the abode for Gods ([devas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deva_\(Hinduism\) "Deva (Hinduism)")). Devas only seek shelter in them and only depend on them.

—  [Taittiriya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taittiriya_Shakha "Taittiriya Shakha") Brahmana, translated by R.L. Kashyap (2017), Ashtaka 1, Prapathaka 2, Anuvaka 5, Verses 1–7 [^54]

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/27/Taittiriya_Samhita_Vedas%2C_Devanagari_script%2C_Sanskrit_pliv.jpg/330px-Taittiriya_Samhita_Vedas%2C_Devanagari_script%2C_Sanskrit_pliv.jpg)

Page of the Taittiriya Samhita.

Ascribed to the sage Tittiri (or Taittiri), the Taittiriya Brahmana of the [Taittiriya Shakha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taittiriya_Shakha "Taittiriya Shakha") consists of three *Ashtakas* (books or parts) of commentaries on the performance of [Vedic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedas "Vedas") [sacrificial rituals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yajna "Yajna"), [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy "Astronomy"), and information about the gods. It is stated by the [Indira Gandhi National Centre for the Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indira_Gandhi_National_Centre_for_the_Arts "Indira Gandhi National Centre for the Arts") (IGNCA) to be 'mixed of [mantras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mantra "Mantra") and Brahmans... composed in poetic and prose manner'.[^55]

M. Winternitz adds that the "Taittiriya-Brahmana of the Black [Yajurveda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yajurveda "Yajurveda") is nothing but a continuation of the Taittiriya-Samhita, for the Brahmanas were already included in the Samhitas of the Black Yajurveda. The Taittiriya-Brahmana, therefore, contains only later additions to the Samhita." [^1]

According to the [Monier-Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monier_Monier-Williams "Monier Monier-Williams") [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit "Sanskrit") Dictionary, Taittiri was a disciple of [Yaska](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yaska "Yaska") (300–400 BCE),[^56] although according to the [Vishnu Purana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vishnu_Purana "Vishnu Purana") (Book 3, Chapter 5), Taittiri and Yaska were disciples of [Vaiśampáyana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaisampayana "Vaisampayana") (500–600 BCE).[^57] According to [H.H. Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horace_Hayman_Wilson "Horace Hayman Wilson"), in the [Anukramańí](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anukrama%E1%B9%87%C4%AB "Anukramaṇī") (index of the black Yajurveda), it 'is there said that Vaiśampáyana taught it to Yaska, who taught it to Tittiri, who also became a teacher; whence the term Taittiríya, for a grammatical rule explains it to mean, 'The Taittiríyas are those who read what was said or repeated by Tittiri'.' [^58]

#### Taittiriya Chardi

Although the Taittiriya Chardi Brahmana is mentioned (i.e. listed) by academics such as S. Shri [^14] and S.N. Nair,[^59] no further information could be found.

#### Taittiriya Pravargya

The Taittiriya Pravargya is a commentary on the Pravargya ritual, contained in the [Taittiriya Aranyaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taittiriya_Shakha "Taittiriya Shakha"). This is not listed or referred to as a Brahmana in the works cited.

Dalal states that the Vadhula (or Anvakhyana) Brahmana of the Vadhula [Shakha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakha "Shakha") is 'a Brahmana type of text, though it is actually part of the Vadhula Shrauta Sutra'.[^13] However, B.B. Chaubey states that about 'the nature of the text there has been confusion whether *VadhAnva* \[Vadhula Anvakhyana Brahmana\] is a Brahmana, or an Anubrahmana \['work resembling a brahmana' or 'according to the brahmana'\],[^60] or an Anvakhyana \['explanation keeping close to the text' or 'minute account or statement'\].[^61] When Caland found some newly discovered MSS \[manuscript\] of the Vadhula School he was not sure about the nature of the text. Because of the composite nature of the MS \[manuscript\] he took the text as part of the Srautasurta of the Vadhulas. However, he was not unaware of the Brahmanic character of the text... according to Caland, the word Anvakhyana was given as a specific name to the Brahmanas, or Brahmana-like passages of the Vadhulasutra'.[^62]

### Gopatha Brahmana

> These (waters) which having encircled the seed, the ocean, rested into (it); those (waters) flowed together towards the east, south, west, and north. In that those (waters) flowed together (towards it), therefore, it is called samudra (ocean). Frightened they said to the lord, alone may we choose as King. And in that having encircled (the ocean), rested into (it), that became Varana. Him who is (really) Varana they call mystically [Varuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varuna "Varuna"). Gods are fond of mystical (presentation) as it were, and haters of direct (presentation).

— Gopatha Brahmana, translated by H.C. Patyal (1969), Prapathaka I.1.7 [^64]

Bloomfield states that the [Gopatha Brahmana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gopatha_Brahmana "Gopatha Brahmana") 'does not favour us with a report of the name of its author or authors. it is divided into two parts, the purva-brahmana in five prapathakas (chapters), and the uttara-brahmana in six prapathakas. The purva shows considerable originality, especially when it is engaged in the glorification of the [Atharvan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atharvan "Atharvan") and its priests; this is indeed its main purpose. Its materials are by no means all of the usual Brahmana-character; they broach frequently upon the domain of [Upanishad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upanishads "Upanishads")... The uttara has certainly some, though probably very few original sections'.[^63]

S.S. Bahulkar states that the 'Gopatha Brahmana (GB.) is the only brahmana text of AV \[[Atharvaveda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atharvaveda "Atharvaveda")\], belonging to both the recensions \[[Shakhas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakha "Shakha")\], viz. Saunaka and Paippalada'.[^65] Dalal agrees, stating the 'aim of this Brahmana seems to be to incorporate the Atharva \[Veda\] in the Vedic ritual, and bring it in line with the other three Vedas. This Brahmana is the same for the Paippalada and Shaunaka shakhas, and is the only existing Brahmana of the Artharva Veda'.[^13] C. Majumdar states that 'although classed as a Brahmana, \[it\] really belongs to the [Vedanga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedanga "Vedanga") literature, and is a very late work'.[^29]

## Lost Brahmanas

[M Haug](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Haug "Martin Haug") states that there 'must have been, as we may learn from [Panini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C4%81%E1%B9%87ini "Pāṇini") and [Patanjali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patanjali "Patanjali") 's [Mahabhasya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mah%C4%81bh%C4%81%E1%B9%A3ya "Mahābhāṣya"), a much larger number of Brahmanas belonging to each [Veda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedas "Vedas"); and even Sayana, who lived only about four \[now five\] hundred years ago, was acquainted with more than we have now'.[^7]

S. Shrava states that 'Innumerable manuscripts of the valuable \[Vedic\] literature have been lost due to atrocities of the rulers and invaders, ravages of time, and utter disregard and negligence. These factors contributed to the loss of hundreds of manuscripts. Once their number was more than a few hundred. Had these been available today the ambiguity in the interpretation of [Vedic hymns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samhita "Samhita") could not have crept in'.[^8] Based on references in other Sanskrit literature, Shrava lists many of these lost works:[^8]
