The **Upanishads** ([/ ʊ ˈ p ʌ n ɪ ʃ ə d z /](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English");[^11] [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit_language "Sanskrit language"): उपनिषद्, [IAST](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IAST "IAST"): *Upaniṣad*, pronounced [\[ˈupɐniʂɐd\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Sanskrit "Help:IPA/Sanskrit")) are [late Vedic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedic_period "Vedic period") and post-Vedic Sanskrit texts that "document the transition from the archaic ritualism of the [Veda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedas "Vedas") into new religious ideas and institutions" [^12] and the emergence of the central religious concepts of [Hinduism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism "Hinduism").[^12] [^1] They are the most recent addition to the [Vedas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedas "Vedas"), the oldest scriptures of Hinduism, and deal with [meditation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meditation "Meditation"), [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_philosophy "Indian philosophy"), [consciousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consciousness "Consciousness"), and [ontological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontology "Ontology") knowledge. Earlier parts of the Vedas dealt with mantras, benedictions, rituals, ceremonies, and sacrifices.[^13] [^14] [^15]

While among the most important literature in the history of Indian religions and culture, the Upanishads document a wide variety of "rites, incantations, and esoteric knowledge" [^16] departing from Vedic ritualism and interpreted in various ways in the later commentarial traditions. The Upanishads are widely known, and their diverse ideas, interpreted in various ways, informed later traditions of Hinduism.[^1] The central concern of all Upanishads is to discover the relations between ritual, cosmic realities (including gods), and the human body/person,[^17] postulating [Ātman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%80tman_\(Hinduism\) "Ātman (Hinduism)") and [Brahman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahman "Brahman") as the "summit of the hierarchically arranged and interconnected universe",[^18] [^19] [^20] but various ideas about the relation between Atman and Brahman can be found.[^20] [^2]

108 Upanishads are known, of which the first dozen or so are the oldest and most important and are referred to as the principal or main (*[mukhya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mukhya "Mukhya")*) Upanishads.[^21] [^22] The *mukhya* Upanishads are found mostly in the concluding part of the *[Brahmanas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmanas "Brahmanas")* and *[Aranyakas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aranyakas "Aranyakas")* [^23] and were, for centuries, memorized by each generation and passed down [orally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oral_tradition "Oral tradition"). The *mukhya* Upanishads predate the [Common Era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Era "Common Era"), but there is no scholarly consensus on their date, or even on which ones are pre- or post-Buddhist. The [*Brhadaranyaka*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brhadaranyaka_Upanishad "Brhadaranyaka Upanishad") is seen as particularly ancient by modern scholars.[^24] [^25] [^26] Of the remainder, 95 Upanishads are part of the [Muktikā](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muktik%C4%81 "Muktikā") canon, composed from about the last centuries of 1st-millennium BCE through about 15th-century CE.[^27] [^28] New Upanishads, beyond the 108 in the Muktika canon, continued to be composed through the early modern and modern era,[^29] though often dealing with subjects that are unconnected to the Vedas.[^30] The *mukhya* Upanishads, along with the *[Bhagavad Gita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhagavad_Gita "Bhagavad Gita")* and the [Brahmasutra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmasutra "Brahmasutra") (known collectively as the *[Prasthanatrayi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prasthanatrayi "Prasthanatrayi")*),[^31] are interpreted in divergent ways in the several later schools of [Vedanta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedanta "Vedanta").[^20] [^3] [^32]

Translations of the Upanishads in the early 19th century started to attract attention from a Western audience. German philosopher [Arthur Schopenhauer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Schopenhauer "Arthur Schopenhauer") was deeply impressed by the Upanishads and called them "the most profitable and elevating reading which... is possible in the world." [^33] Modern era [Indologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indology "Indology") have discussed the similarities between the fundamental concepts in the Upanishads and the works of major [Western philosophers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_philosophers "Western philosophers").[^34] [^35] [^36]

## Etymology

The [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit "Sanskrit") term **Upaniṣad** originally meant “connection” or “equivalence",[^37] but came to be understood as "sitting near a teacher," [^37] from *upa* "by" and *ni-ṣad* "sit down",[^38] "sitting down near", referring to the student sitting down near the teacher while receiving spiritual knowledge (Gurumukh).[^39] Other dictionary meanings include "esoteric doctrine" and "secret doctrine". [Monier-Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monier-Williams "Monier-Williams") ' *Sanskrit Dictionary* notes – "According to native authorities, Upanishad means setting to rest ignorance by revealing the knowledge of the supreme spirit." [^40]

[Adi Shankaracharya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adi_Shankara "Adi Shankara") explains in his commentary on the [*Kaṭha*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katha_Upanishad "Katha Upanishad") and [Brihadaranyaka Upanishad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brihadaranyaka_Upanishad "Brihadaranyaka Upanishad") that the word means *Ātmavidyā*, that is, "knowledge of the [self](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atman_\(Hinduism\) "Atman (Hinduism)") ", or *Brahmavidyā* "knowledge of Brahman". The word appears in the verses of many Upanishads, such as the fourth verse of the 13th volume in the first chapter of the Chandogya Upanishad. [Max Müller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_M%C3%BCller "Max Müller") as well as [Paul Deussen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Deussen "Paul Deussen") translate the word *Upanishad* in these verses as "secret doctrine",[^41] [^42] Robert Hume translates it as "mystic meaning",[^43] while [Patrick Olivelle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Olivelle "Patrick Olivelle") translates it as "hidden connections".[^44]

## Development

### Authorship

The authorship of most Upanishads is unknown. [Sarvapalli Radhakrishnan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarvapalli_Radhakrishnan "Sarvapalli Radhakrishnan") states, "almost all the early literature of India was anonymous, we do not know the names of the authors of the Upanishads".[^45] The ancient Upanishads are embedded in the Vedas, the oldest of Hinduism's religious scriptures, which some traditionally consider to be *[apauruṣeya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apauru%E1%B9%A3eya "Apauruṣeya")*, which means "not of a man, superhuman" [^46] and "impersonal, authorless".[^47] [^48] [^49] The Vedic texts assert that they were skillfully created by *[Rishis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rishi "Rishi")* (sages), after inspired creativity, just as a carpenter builds a chariot.[^50]

The various philosophical theories in the early Upanishads have been attributed to famous sages such as [Yajnavalkya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yajnavalkya "Yajnavalkya"), [Uddalaka Aruni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uddalaka_Aruni "Uddalaka Aruni"), [Shvetaketu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shvetaketu "Shvetaketu"), [Shandilya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandilya_\(Rishi\) "Sandilya (Rishi)"), Aitareya, Balaki, [Pippalada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pippalada "Pippalada"), and [Sanatkumara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanatkumara "Sanatkumara").[^45] [^51] Women, such as Maitreyi and [Gargi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gargi_Vachaknavi "Gargi Vachaknavi"), participate in the dialogues and are also credited in the early Upanishads.[^52] There are some exceptions to the anonymous tradition of the Upanishads. The [Shvetashvatara Upanishad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shvetashvatara_Upanishad "Shvetashvatara Upanishad"), for example, includes closing credits to sage *Shvetashvatara*, and he is considered the author of the Upanishad.[^53]

Many scholars believe that early Upanishads were interpolated [^54] and expanded over time. There are differences within manuscripts of the same Upanishad discovered in different parts of South Asia, differences in non-Sanskrit version of the texts that have survived, and differences within each text in terms of meter,[^55] style, grammar and structure.[^56] [^57] The existing texts are believed to be the work of many authors.[^58]

### Chronology

Scholars are uncertain about when the Upanishads were composed.[^59] The chronology of the early Upanishads is difficult to resolve, states philosopher and [Sanskritist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskritist "Sanskritist") Stephen Phillips,[^21] because all opinions rest on scanty evidence and analysis of archaism, style and repetitions across texts, and are driven by assumptions about likely evolution of ideas, and presumptions about which philosophy might have influenced which other Indian philosophies. Indologist [Patrick Olivelle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Olivelle "Patrick Olivelle") says that "in spite of claims made by some, in reality, any dating of these documents \[early Upanishads\] that attempts a precision closer than a few centuries is as stable as a house of cards".[^24]

Some scholars have tried to analyse similarities between Hindu Upanishads and Buddhist literature to establish chronology for the Upanishads.[^25] Precise dates are impossible, and most scholars give only broad ranges encompassing various centuries. [Gavin Flood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gavin_Flood "Gavin Flood") states that "the Upanisads are not a homogeneous group of texts. Even the older texts were composed over a wide expanse of time from about 600 to 300 BCE." [^60] Stephen Phillips places the early or "principal" Upanishads in the 800 to 300 BCE range.[^21]

[Patrick Olivelle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Olivelle "Patrick Olivelle"), a Sanskrit [Philologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philology "Philology") and [Indologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indology "Indology"), gives the following chronology for the early Upanishads, also called the [Principal Upanishads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_Upanishads "Principal Upanishads"):[^59] [^24]

- The [*Brhadaranyaka*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brhadaranyaka_Upanishad "Brhadaranyaka Upanishad") and the [*Chandogya*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chandogya_Upanishad "Chandogya Upanishad") are the two earliest Upanishads. They are edited texts, some of whose sources are much older than others. The two texts are pre-Buddhist; they may be placed in the 7th to 6th centuries BCE, give or take a century or so.[^61] [^25]
- The three other early prose Upanishads— *Taittiriya, Aitareya*, and *Kausitaki* come next; all are probably pre-Buddhist and can be assigned to the 6th to 5th centuries BCE.[^62]
- The Kena is the oldest of the verse Upanishads followed by probably the Katha, Isa, [Svetasvatara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shvetashvatara_Upanishad "Shvetashvatara Upanishad"), and Mundaka. All these Upanishads were composed probably in the last few centuries BCE.[^63] According to Olivelle, "All exhibit strong [theistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theism "Theism") tendencies and are probably the earliest literary products of the theistic tradition, whose later literature includes the Bhagavad Gita and the Puranas." [^64]
- The two late prose Upanishads, the Prasna and the Mandukya, cannot be much older than the beginning of the common era.[^59] [^24]

Meanwhile, the Indologist [Johannes Bronkhorst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannes_Bronkhorst "Johannes Bronkhorst") argues for a later date for the Upanishads than has generally been accepted. Bronkhorst places even the oldest of the Upanishads, such as the *Brhadaranyaka* as possibly still being composed at "a date close to [Katyayana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C4%81ty%C4%81yana "Kātyāyana") and [Patañjali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mah%C4%81bh%C4%81%E1%B9%A3ya "Mahābhāṣya") \[the grammarian\]" (i.e., c. 2nd century BCE).[^26]

The later Upanishads, numbering about 95, also called minor Upanishads, are dated from the late 1st-millennium BCE to mid 2nd-millennium CE.[^27] [Gavin Flood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gavin_Flood "Gavin Flood") dates many of the twenty [Yoga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoga "Yoga") Upanishads to be probably from the 100 BCE to 300 CE period.[^28] [Patrick Olivelle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Olivelle "Patrick Olivelle") and other scholars date seven of the twenty [Sannyasa Upanishads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sannyasa#Sannyasa_Upanishads "Sannyasa") to likely have been complete sometime between the last centuries of the 1st-millennium BCE to 300 CE.[^27] About half of the Sannyasa Upanishads were likely composed in 14th- to 15th-century CE.[^27]

### Geography

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/51/Late_Vedic_Culture_%281100-500_BCE%29.png/960px-Late_Vedic_Culture_%281100-500_BCE%29.png)

Geography of the Late Vedic Period

The general area of the composition of the early Upanishads is considered as northern India. The region is bounded on the west by the upper Indus valley, on the east by lower Ganges region, on the north by the Himalayan foothills, and on the south by the Vindhya mountain range.[^24] Scholars are reasonably sure that the early Upanishads were produced at the geographical center of ancient Brahmanism, [Kuru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuru_Kingdom "Kuru Kingdom") - [Panchala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panchala "Panchala"), and [Kosala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kosala "Kosala") - [Videha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videha "Videha"), a "frontier region" of Brahmanism, together with the areas immediately to the south and west of these.[^65] This region covers modern [Bihar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bihar "Bihar"), [Nepal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nepal "Nepal"), [Uttar Pradesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uttar_Pradesh "Uttar Pradesh"), [Uttarakhand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uttarakhand "Uttarakhand"), [Himachal Pradesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himachal_Pradesh "Himachal Pradesh"), [Haryana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haryana "Haryana"), eastern [Rajasthan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rajasthan "Rajasthan"), and northern [Madhya Pradesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madhya_Pradesh "Madhya Pradesh").[^24]

While significant attempts have been made recently to identify the exact locations of the individual Upanishads, the results are tentative. Witzel identifies the center of activity in the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad as the area of Videha, whose king, Janaka, features prominently in the Upanishad.[^66] The Chandogya Upanishad was probably composed in a more western than eastern location in the Indian subcontinent, possibly somewhere in the western region of the Kuru-Panchala country.[^67]

Compared to the Principal Upanishads, the new Upanishads recorded in the *Muktikā* belong to an entirely different region, probably southern India, and are considerably relatively recent.[^68] In the fourth chapter of the Kaushitaki Upanishad, a location named Kashi (modern [Varanasi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varanasi "Varanasi")) is mentioned.[^24]

## Classification

There are more than 200 known *Upanishads*, one of which, the **[Muktikā](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muktik%C4%81 "Muktikā")** Upanishad, predates 1656 CE [^69] and contains a list of 108 canonical Upanishads,[^70] including itself as the last. These are further divided into Upanishads associated with [Shaktism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shaktism "Shaktism") (goddess Shakti), [Sannyasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sannyasa "Sannyasa") (renunciation, monastic life), [Shaivism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shaivism "Shaivism") (god Shiva), [Vaishnavism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaishnavism "Vaishnavism") (god Vishnu), [Yoga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoga "Yoga"), and *Sāmānya* (general, sometimes referred to as Samanya-Vedanta).[^71] [^72]

Some of the Upanishads are categorized as "sectarian" since they present their ideas through a particular god or goddess of a specific Hindu tradition such as Vishnu, Shiva, Shakti, or a combination of these such as the [Skanda Upanishad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skanda_Upanishad "Skanda Upanishad"). These traditions sought to link their texts as Vedic, by asserting their texts to be an Upanishad, thereby a *[Śruti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%9Aruti "Śruti")*.[^73] Most of these sectarian Upanishads, for example the [Rudrahridaya Upanishad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudrahridaya_Upanishad "Rudrahridaya Upanishad") and the [Mahanarayana Upanishad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahanarayana_Upanishad "Mahanarayana Upanishad"), assert that all the Hindu gods and goddesses are the same, all an aspect and manifestation of [Brahman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahman "Brahman"), the Vedic concept for metaphysical ultimate reality before and after the creation of the Universe.[^74] [^75]

### Principal Upanishads

The Principal Upanishads, also known as the *Mukhya Upanishads*, can be grouped into periods. Of the early periods are the *Brihadaranyaka* and the *Chandogya*, the oldest.[^76] [^4]

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/17/MS_Indic_37%2C_Isa_upanisad._Wellcome_L0027330.jpg/500px-MS_Indic_37%2C_Isa_upanisad._Wellcome_L0027330.jpg)

A page of Isha Upanishad manuscript

The Aitareya, Kauṣītaki and Taittirīya Upanishads may date to as early as the mid-1st millennium BCE, while the remnant date from between roughly the 4th to 1st centuries BCE, roughly contemporary with the earliest portions of the [Sanskrit epics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit_epics "Sanskrit epics"). One chronology assumes that the *Aitareya, Taittiriya, Kausitaki, Mundaka, Prasna*, and *Katha Upanishads* has Buddha's influence, and is consequently placed after the 5th century BCE, while another proposal questions this assumption and dates it independent of Buddha's date of birth. The *Kena*, *Mandukya,* and *Isa Upanishads* are typically placed after these Principal Upanishads, but other scholars date these differently.[^25] Not much is known about the authors except for those, like Yajnavalkayva and Uddalaka, mentioned in the texts.[^23] A few women discussants, such as Gargi and Maitreyi, the wife of Yajnavalkayva,[^78] also feature occasionally.

Each of the principal *Upanishads* can be associated with one of the schools of exegesis of the four Vedas (*[shakhas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakha "Shakha")*).[^79] Many Shakhas are said to have existed, of which only a few remain. The new *Upanishads* often have little relation to the Vedic corpus and have not been cited or commented upon by any great Vedanta philosopher: their language differs from that of the classic *Upanishads*, being less subtle and more formalized. As a result, they are not difficult to comprehend for the modern reader.[^80]

### New Upanishads

There is no fixed list of the *Upanishads* as newer ones, beyond the Muktika anthology of 108 Upanishads, have continued to be discovered and composed.[^81] In 1908, for example, four previously unknown Upanishads were discovered in newly found manuscripts, and these were named *Bashkala*, *Chhagaleya*, *Arsheya*, and *Saunaka*, by [Friedrich Schrader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Schrader "Friedrich Schrader"),[^82] who attributed them to the first prose period of the Upanishads.[^83] The text of three of them, namely the *Chhagaleya*, *Arsheya*, and *Saunaka*, were incomplete and inconsistent, likely poorly maintained or corrupted.[^83]

Ancient Upanishads have long enjoyed a revered position in Hindu traditions, and authors of numerous sectarian texts have tried to benefit from this reputation by naming their texts as Upanishads.[^84] These "new Upanishads" number in the hundreds, cover diverse range of topics from physiology [^85] to renunciation [^86] to sectarian theories.[^84] They were composed between the last centuries of the 1st millennium BCE through the early modern era (~1600 CE).[^84] [^86] While over two dozen of the minor Upanishads are dated to pre-3rd century CE,[^27] [^28] many of these new texts under the title of "Upanishads" originated in the first half of the 2nd millennium CE,[^84] they are not Vedic texts, and some do not deal with themes found in the Vedic Upanishads.[^30]

The main [Shakta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shaktism "Shaktism") Upanishads, for example, mostly discuss doctrinal and interpretative differences between the two principal sects of a major [Tantric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tantra "Tantra") form of Shaktism called [Shri Vidya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shri_Vidya "Shri Vidya") [upasana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upasana "Upasana"). The many extant lists of authentic *Shakta Upaniṣads* vary, reflecting the sect of their compilers, so that they yield no evidence of their "location" in Tantric tradition, impeding correct interpretation. The Tantra content of these texts also weaken its identity as an Upaniṣad for non-Tantrikas. Sectarian texts such as these do not enjoy status as [shruti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shruti "Shruti") and thus the authority of the new Upanishads as scripture is not accepted in Hinduism.[^87]

All Upanishads are associated with one of the four Vedas— [Rigveda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigveda "Rigveda"), [Samaveda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samaveda "Samaveda"), [Yajurveda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yajurveda "Yajurveda") (there are two primary versions or *Samhitas* of the Yajurveda: [Shukla Yajurveda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Yajurveda "White Yajurveda"), [Krishna Yajurveda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Yajurveda "Black Yajurveda")), and [Atharvaveda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atharvaveda "Atharvaveda").[^88] During the modern era, the ancient Upanishads that were embedded texts in the Vedas, were detached from the [Brahmana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmana "Brahmana") and [Aranyaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aranyaka "Aranyaka") layers of Vedic text, compiled into separate texts and these were then gathered into [anthologies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthology "Anthology") of the Upanishads.[^84] These lists associated each Upanishad with one of the four Vedas. Many such lists exist but they are inconsistent across India in terms of which Upanishads are included and how the newer Upanishads are assigned to the ancient Vedas. In south India, the collected list based on Muktika Upanishad,[^5] and published in [Telugu language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telugu_language "Telugu language"), became the most common by the 19th-century and this is a list of 108 Upanishads.[^84] [^89] In north India, a list of 52 Upanishads has been most common.[^84]

## Philosophy

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b4/Wassertropfen.jpg/500px-Wassertropfen.jpg)

Impact of a drop of water, a common analogy for Brahman and the Ātman

The central concern of all Upanishads is to discover the relations between ritual, cosmic realities (including gods), and the human body/person,[^17] postulating [Ātman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%80tman_\(Hinduism\) "Ātman (Hinduism)") and [Brahman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahman "Brahman") as the "summit of the hierarchically arranged and interconnected universe," [^18] [^19] [^20] but various ideas about the relation between Atman and Brahman can be found.[^20] [^2]

The Upanishads reflect a pluralism of worldviews. While some Upanishads have been deemed 'monistic', others, including the [Katha Upanishad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katha_Upanishad "Katha Upanishad"), are [dualistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dualism_\(Indian_philosophy\) "Dualism (Indian philosophy)").[^102] The Maitri is one of the Upanishads that inclines more toward dualism, thus grounding classical [Samkhya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samkhya "Samkhya") and [Yoga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoga_\(philosophy\) "Yoga (philosophy)") schools of Hinduism, in contrast to the non-dualistic Upanishads at the foundation of its Vedanta school.[^103] They contain a plurality of ideas.[^104] [^2]

The Upanishads include sections on philosophical theories that have been at the foundation of Indian traditions. For example, the [Chandogya Upanishad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chandogya_Upanishad "Chandogya Upanishad") includes one of the earliest known declarations of [Ahimsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahimsa "Ahimsa") (non-violence) as an ethical precept.[^105] [^106] Discussion of other ethical premises such as [Damah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperance_\(virtue\)#Hinduism "Temperance (virtue)") (temperance, self-restraint), [Satya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satya "Satya") (truthfulness), [Dāna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C4%81na "Dāna") (charity), [Ārjava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%80rjava "Ārjava") (non-hypocrisy), [Daya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compassion#Hinduism "Compassion") (compassion), and others are found in the oldest Upanishads and many later Upanishads.[^107] [^108] Similarly, the Karma doctrine is presented in the [Brihadaranyaka Upanishad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brihadaranyaka_Upanishad "Brihadaranyaka Upanishad"), which is the oldest Upanishad.[^109]

While the hymns of the Vedas emphasize rituals and the Brahmanas serve as a liturgical manual for those Vedic rituals, the spirit of the Upanishads is inherently opposed to ritual.[^110] The older Upanishads launch attacks of increasing intensity on the ritual. Anyone who worships a divinity other than the self is called a domestic animal of the gods in the [Brihadaranyaka Upanishad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brihadaranyaka_Upanishad "Brihadaranyaka Upanishad"). The *Chāndogya* Upanishad parodies those who indulge in the acts of sacrifice by comparing them with a procession of dogs chanting *Om! Let's eat. Om! Let's drink*.[^110]

The [Kaushitaki Upanishad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaushitaki_Upanishad "Kaushitaki Upanishad") asserts that "external rituals such as [Agnihotram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agnihotra "Agnihotra") offered in the morning and in the evening, must be replaced with inner Agnihotram, the ritual of introspection", and that "not rituals, but knowledge should be one's pursuit".[^111] The [Mundaka Upanishad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mundaka_Upanishad "Mundaka Upanishad") declares how man has been called upon, promised benefits for, scared unto and misled into performing sacrifices, oblations and pious works.[^112] Mundaka thereafter asserts this is foolish and frail, by those who encourage it and those who follow it, because it makes no difference to man's current life and after-life, it is like blind men leading the blind, it is a mark of conceit and vain knowledge, ignorant inertia like that of children, a futile useless practice.[^112] [^113] The [Maitri Upanishad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maitrayaniya_Upanishad "Maitrayaniya Upanishad") states,[^114]

> The performance of all the sacrifices, described in the Maitrayana-Brahmana, is to lead up in the end to a knowledge of Brahman, to prepare a man for meditation. Therefore, let such man, after he has laid those fires,[^115] meditate on the Self, to become complete and perfect. But who is to be meditated on?

The opposition to the ritual is not explicit in the oldest Upanishads. On occasions, the Upanishads extend the task of the Aranyakas by making the ritual allegorical and giving it a philosophical meaning. For example, the Brihadaranyaka interprets the practice of horse-sacrifice or *[ashvamedha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashvamedha "Ashvamedha")* allegorically. It states that the over-lordship of the earth may be acquired by sacrificing a horse. It then goes on to say that spiritual autonomy can only be achieved by renouncing the universe which is conceived in the image of a horse.[^110]

In similar fashion, [Vedic gods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigvedic_deities "Rigvedic deities") such as the *Agni*, *Aditya*, *Indra*, *Rudra*, *Visnu*, *Brahma*, and others become equated in the Upanishads to the supreme, immortal, and incorporeal Brahman-Atman of the Upanishads, god becomes synonymous with self, and is declared to be everywhere, inmost being of each human being and within every living creature.[^118] [^119] [^120] The one reality or *ekam sat* of the Vedas becomes the *ekam eva advitiyam* or "the one and only and sans a second" in the Upanishads.[^110] Brahman-Atman and self-realization develops, in the Upanishad, as the means to [moksha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moksha "Moksha") (liberation; freedom in this life or after-life).[^120] [^121] [^122]

According to [Jayatilleke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K._N._Jayatilleke "K. N. Jayatilleke"), the thinkers of Upanishadic texts can be grouped into two categories.[^123] One group, which includes early Upanishads along with some middle and late Upanishads, were composed by metaphysicians who used rational arguments and empirical experience to formulate their speculations and philosophical premises. The second group includes many middle and later Upanishads, where their authors professed theories based on yoga and personal experiences.[^123] Yoga philosophy and practice, adds Jayatilleke, is "not entirely absent in the Early Upanishads".[^123]

The development of thought in these Upanishadic theories contrasted with Buddhism, since the Upanishadic inquiry fails to find an empirical correlate of the assumed Atman, but nevertheless assumes its existence,[^124] "\[reifying\] consciousness as an eternal self." [^125] The Buddhist inquiry "is satisfied with the empirical investigation which shows that no such Atman exists because there is no evidence," states Jayatilleke.[^124]

The Upanishads postulate [Ātman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%80tman_\(Hinduism\) "Ātman (Hinduism)") and [Brahman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahman "Brahman") as the "summit of the hierarchically arranged and interconnected universe." [^18] [^19] [^20] Both have multiple meanings,[^126] and various ideas about the relation between Atman and Brahman can be found.[^20] [^2]

Atman has "a wide range of lexical meanings, including ‘breath’, ‘spirit’, and ‘body’." [^127] In the Upanishads it refers to the body, but also to the essence of the concrete physical human body,[^18] "an essence, a life-force, consciousness, or ultimate reality." [^127] The Chāndogya Upaniṣhad (6.1-16) "offers an organic understanding of ātman, characterizing the self in terms of the life force that animates all living beings," while the Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣhad "characterizes ātman more in terms of consciousness than as a life-giving essence." [^127]

Brahman may refer to a "formulation of truth," but also to "the ultimate and basic essence of the cosmos," standing at the "summit of the hierarchical scheme, or at the bottom as the ultimate foundation of all things." [^126] Brahman is "beyond the reach of human perception and thought." [^128] Atman likewise has multiple meanings, one of them being 'self', the inner essence of a human body/person.[^129] [^130] [^8]

Various ideas about the relation between Atman and Brahman can be found.[^20] [^2] Two distinct, somewhat divergent themes stand out. Older upanishads state that *Atman* is part of Brahman but not identical, while younger Upanishads state that Brahman (Highest Reality, Universal Principle, Being-Consciousness-Bliss) is identical with *Atman*.[^131] [^132] The [Brahmasutra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmasutra "Brahmasutra") by Badarayana (c. 100 BCE) synthesized and unified these somewhat conflicting theories. According to Nakamura, the Brahmasutras see Atman and Brahman as both different and not-different, a point of view which came to be called *[bhedabheda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhedabheda "Bhedabheda")* in later times.[^133] According to Koller, the Brahmasutras state that Atman and Brahman are different in some respects particularly during the state of ignorance, but at the deepest level and in the state of self-realization, Atman and Brahman are identical, non-different.[^131] This ancient debate flowered into various dual, non-dual theories in Hinduism.

Two different types of the non-dual Brahman-Atman are presented in the Upanishads, according to Mahadevan. The one in which the non-dual Brahman-Atman is the all-inclusive ground of the universe and another in which empirical, changing reality is an appearance (Maya).[^134]

The Upanishads describe the universe, and the human experience, as an interplay of [Purusha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purusha "Purusha") (the eternal, unchanging principles, consciousness) and [Prakṛti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prak%E1%B9%9Bti "Prakṛti") (the temporary, changing material world, nature).[^135] The former manifests itself as [Ātman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%80tman_\(Hinduism\) "Ātman (Hinduism)") (soul, self), and the latter as [Māyā](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_\(illusion\) "Maya (illusion)"). The Upanishads refer to the knowledge of *Atman* as "true knowledge" (*Vidya*), and the knowledge of *Maya* as "not true knowledge" (*Avidya*, Nescience, lack of awareness, lack of true knowledge).[^136]

Hendrick Vroom explains, "the term *Maya* \[in the Upanishads\] has been translated as 'illusion,' but then it does not concern normal illusion. Here 'illusion' does not mean that the world is not real and simply a figment of the human imagination. *Maya* means that the world is not as it seems; the world that one experiences is misleading as far as its true nature is concerned." [^137] According to [Wendy Doniger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wendy_Doniger "Wendy Doniger"), "to say that the universe is an illusion (māyā) is not to say that it is unreal; it is to say, instead, that it is not what it seems to be, that it is something constantly being made. Māyā not only deceives people about the things they think they know; more basically, it limits their knowledge." [^138]

In the Upanishads, Māyā is the perceived changing reality and it co-exists with Brahman which is the hidden true reality.[^139] [^140] *Maya*, or "illusion", is an important idea in the Upanishads, because the texts assert that in the human pursuit of blissful and liberating self-knowledge, it is *Maya* which obscures, confuses and distracts an individual.[^141] [^142]

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e3/Raja_Ravi_Varma_-_Sankaracharya.jpg/330px-Raja_Ravi_Varma_-_Sankaracharya.jpg)

Adi Shankara, expounder of Advaita Vedanta and commentator ( bhashya ) on the Upanishads

The Upanishads form one of the three main sources for all schools of Vedanta, together with the Bhagavad Gita and the [Brahmasutras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmasutra "Brahmasutra").[^143] Due to the wide variety of philosophical teachings contained in the Upanishads, various interpretations could be grounded on the Upanishads.[^2] [^9] The schools of Vedānta seek to answer questions about the relation between atman and Brahman, and the relation between Brahman and the world.[^144] The schools of Vedanta are named after the relation they see between atman and Brahman:[^145]

- According to [Advaita Vedanta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advaita_Vedanta "Advaita Vedanta"), there is no difference.[^145]
- According to [Vishishtadvaita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vishishtadvaita "Vishishtadvaita") the jīvātman is a part of Brahman, and hence is similar, but not identical.
- According to [Dvaita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dvaita "Dvaita"), all individual souls (jīvātmans) and matter as eternal and mutually separate entities.

Other schools of Vedanta include [Nimbarkacharya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nimbarkacharya "Nimbarkacharya") 's *[Svabhavika Bhedabheda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Svabhavika_Bhedabheda "Svabhavika Bhedabheda")*, [Vallabha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vallabha "Vallabha") 's *Suddhadvaita*, and Chaitanya's *Acintya [Bhedabheda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhedabheda "Bhedabheda")*.[^146] The philosopher [Adi Shankara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adi_Shankara "Adi Shankara") has provided commentaries on 11 [mukhya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mukhya "Mukhya") Upanishads.[^147]

### Advaita Vedanta

Advaita literally means non-duality, and it is a [monistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monistic "Monistic") system of thought.[^148] It deals with the non-dual nature of [Brahman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahman "Brahman") and Atman. Advaita is considered the most influential sub-school of the *Vedanta* school of Hindu philosophy.[^148] Gaudapada was the first person to expound the basic principles of the Advaita philosophy in a commentary on the conflicting statements of the Upanishads.[^149] Gaudapada's Advaita ideas were further developed by [Shankara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adi_Shankara "Adi Shankara") (8th century CE).[^150] [^151] King states that Gaudapada's main work, Māṇḍukya Kārikā, is infused with philosophical terminology of Buddhism, and uses Buddhist arguments and analogies.[^152] King also suggests that there are clear differences between Shankara's writings and the *Brahmasutra*,[^150] [^151] and many ideas of Shankara are at odds with those in the Upanishads.[^153] Radhakrishnan, on the other hand, suggests that Shankara's views of Advaita were straightforward developments of the Upanishads and the *Brahmasutra*,[^154] and many ideas of Shankara derive from the Upanishads.[^155]

Shankara in his discussions of the Advaita Vedanta philosophy referred to the early Upanishads to explain the key difference between Hinduism and Buddhism, stating that Hinduism asserts that Atman (soul, self) exists, whereas Buddhism asserts that there is no soul, no self.[^156] [^157] [^158]

Shankara used four sentences from the Upanishads, called the [Mahāvākyas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahavakyas "Mahavakyas") (Great Sayings), to establish the identity of Atman and Brahman as scriptural truth:

- ["Prajñānam brahma"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mah%C4%81v%C4%81kyas#Praj%C3%B1%C4%81nam_Brahma "Mahāvākyas") - "Consciousness is Brahman" ([Aitareya Upanishad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aitareya_Upanishad "Aitareya Upanishad")) [^159]
- "Aham brahmāsmi" - "I am Brahman" ([Brihadaranyaka Upanishad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brihadaranyaka_Upanishad "Brihadaranyaka Upanishad")) [^160]
- ["Tat tvam asi"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tat_Tvam_Asi "Tat Tvam Asi") - "That Thou art" ([Chandogya Upanishad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chandogya_Upanishad "Chandogya Upanishad")) [^161]
- "Ayamātmā brahma" - "This Atman is Brahman" ([Mandukya Upanishad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandukya_Upanishad "Mandukya Upanishad")) [^162]

### Bhedabheda

Vijñānabhikṣu countered Advaita emphasis on non-difference of the self and Brahman by pointing to statements from the Upanishads that support difference.[^163]

### Vishishtadvaita

[Ramanuja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramanuja "Ramanuja") (1017–1137 CE), the main proponent of the Vishishtadvaita philosophy, disagreed with Adi Shankara and the Advaita school.[^164] Visistadvaita is a synthetic philosophy bridging the monistic Advaita and theistic Dvaita systems of Vedanta.[^165] Ramanuja frequently cited the Upanishads, and stated that Vishishtadvaita is grounded in the Upanishads.[^166] [^167]

Ramanuja's Vishishtadvaita interpretation of the Upanishads is that of qualified [monism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monism "Monism").[^168] [^169] Ramanuja interprets the Upanishadic literature to be teaching a body-soul theory, states Jeaneane Fowler – a professor of Philosophy and Religious Studies, where the Brahman is the dweller in all things, yet also distinct and beyond all things, as the soul, the inner controller, the immortal.[^167] The Upanishads, according to the Vishishtadvaita school, teach individual souls to be of the same quality as the Brahman, but quantitatively distinct.[^170] [^171] [^172]

In the Vishishtadvaita school, the Upanishads are interpreted to be teaching about [Ishvara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ishvara "Ishvara") (Vishnu), who is the seat of all auspicious qualities, with all of the empirically perceived world as the body of God who dwells in everything.[^167] The school recommends a devotion to godliness and constant remembrance of the beauty and love of a personal god. This ultimately leads one to the oneness with abstract Brahman.[^173] [^174] [^175] The Brahman in the Upanishads is a living reality, states Fowler, and "the Atman of all things and all beings" in Ramanuja's interpretation.[^167]

### Dvaita

The Dvaita school was founded by [Madhvacharya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madhvacharya "Madhvacharya") (1199–1278 CE).[^176] It is regarded as a strongly theistic philosophic exposition of the Upanishads.[^165] Madhvacharya, much like Adi Shankara claims for Advaita, and Ramanuja claims for Vishishtadvaita, states that his theistic Dvaita Vedanta is grounded in the Upanishads.[^166]

According to the Dvaita school, states Fowler, the "Upanishads that speak of the soul as Brahman, speak of resemblance and not identity".[^177] Madhvacharya interprets the Upanishadic teachings of the self becoming one with Brahman, as "entering into Brahman", just like a drop enters an ocean. This to the Dvaita school implies duality and dependence, where Brahman and Atman are different realities. Brahman is a separate, independent and supreme reality in the Upanishads, Atman only resembles the Brahman in limited, inferior, dependent manner according to [Madhvacharya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madhvacharya "Madhvacharya").[^177] [^178] [^179]

Ramanuja's Vishishtadvaita school and Shankara's Advaita school are both nondualism Vedanta schools,[^173] both are premised on the assumption that all souls can hope for and achieve the state of blissful liberation; in contrast, Madhvacharya believed that some souls are eternally doomed and damned.[^180] [^181]

Several scholars have recognised parallels between the philosophy of [Pythagoras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagoras "Pythagoras") and [Plato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato "Plato") and that of the Upanishads, including their ideas on [sources of knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemology "Epistemology"), concept of justice and path to salvation, and Plato's [allegory of the cave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allegory_of_the_cave "Allegory of the cave"). Platonic psychology with its divisions of reason, spirit and appetite, also bears resemblance to the three *[Guṇas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gu%E1%B9%87a "Guṇa")* in the Indian philosophy of [Samkhya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samkhya "Samkhya").[^182] [^183] [^10]

Various mechanisms for such a transmission of knowledge have been conjectured including Pythagoras traveling as far as India; Indian philosophers visiting Athens and meeting [Socrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates "Socrates"); Plato encountering the ideas when in exile in Syracuse; or, intermediated through Persia.[^182] [^185]

However, other scholars, such as [Arthur Berriedale Keith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Berriedale_Keith "Arthur Berriedale Keith"), [J. Burnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Burnet_\(classicist\) "John Burnet (classicist)") and [A. R. Wadia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ardeshir_Ruttonji_Wadia "Ardeshir Ruttonji Wadia"), believe that the two systems developed independently. They note that there is no historical evidence of the philosophers of the two schools meeting, and point out significant differences in the stage of development, orientation and goals of the two philosophical systems. Wadia writes that Plato's metaphysics were rooted in *this* life and his primary aim was to develop an ideal state.[^183] In contrast, Upanishadic focus was the individual, the self (atman, soul), self-knowledge, and the means of an individual's [moksha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moksha "Moksha") (freedom, liberation in this life or after-life).[^186] [^187]

## Translations

The Upanishads have been translated into various languages including [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_language "Persian language"), [Italian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_language "Italian language"), [Urdu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urdu "Urdu"), [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language "French language"), [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin "Latin"), [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language "German language"), [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language "English language"), [Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_language "Dutch language"), [Polish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_language "Polish language"), [Japanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_language "Japanese language"), [Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_language "Spanish language") and [Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_language "Russian language").[^188] The [Mughal Emperor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_emperors "Mughal emperors") [Akbar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akbar "Akbar") 's reign (1556–1586) saw the first translations of the Upanishads into Persian.[^189] [^190] His great-grandson, [Dara Shukoh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dara_Shukoh "Dara Shukoh"), produced a collection called *[Sirr-i-Akbar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sirr-i-Akbar "Sirr-i-Akbar")* in 1656, wherein 50 Upanishads were translated from Sanskrit into [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_language "Persian language").[^191]

[Anquetil-Duperron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Hyacinthe_Anquetil-Duperron "Abraham Hyacinthe Anquetil-Duperron"), a French Orientalist, received a manuscript of the *Oupanekhat* and translated the Persian version into French and Latin, publishing the Latin translation in two volumes in 1801–1802 as *Oupneck'hat*.[^191] [^189] The French translation was never published.[^192] More recently, several translations in French of some Upanishads or the whole of 108 have been published: by indianists [Louis Renou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Renou "Louis Renou"), *Kausitaki, Svetasvatra, Prasna, Taittiriya Upanisads*, 1948;[^193] [Jean Varenne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Varenne "Jean Varenne"), *Mahâ-Nârâyana Upanisad*, 1960,[^194] and *Sept Upanishads*, 1981;[^195] Alyette Degrâces-Fadh, *Samnyâsa-Upanisad (Upanisad du renoncement)*, 1989;[^196] Martine Buttex, *Les 108 Upanishads* (full translation), 2012.[^197]

The Latin version was the initial introduction of the Upanishadic thought to Western scholars.[^198] However, according to Deussen, the Persian translators took great liberties in translating the text and at times changed the meaning.[^199]

The first Sanskrit-to-English translation of the [Aitareya Upanishad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aitareya_Upanishad "Aitareya Upanishad") was made by [Colebrooke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Thomas_Colebrooke "Henry Thomas Colebrooke") [^200] in 1805, and the first English translation of the [Kena Upanishad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kena_Upanishad "Kena Upanishad") was made by [Rammohun Roy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rammohun_Roy "Rammohun Roy") in 1816.[^201] [^202]

The first German translation appeared in 1832 and Roer's English version appeared in 1853. However, Max Mueller's 1879 and 1884 editions were the first systematic English treatment to include the 12 Principal Upanishads.[^188] Other major translations of the Upanishads have been by Robert Ernest Hume (13 Principal Upanishads),[^203] [Paul Deussen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Deussen "Paul Deussen") (60 Upanishads),[^204] [Sarvepalli Radhakrishnan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarvepalli_Radhakrishnan "Sarvepalli Radhakrishnan") (18 Upanishads),[^205] [Patrick Olivelle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Olivelle "Patrick Olivelle") (32 Upanishads in two books) [^206] [^207] and Bhānu Swami (13 Upanishads with commentaries of Vaiṣṇava ācāryas). Olivelle's translation won the 1998 A.K. Ramanujan Book Prize for Translation.[^208]

Throughout the 1930s, [Irish poet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Irish_poets "List of Irish poets") [W. B. Yeats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._B._Yeats "W. B. Yeats") worked with the Indian-born [mendicant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sadhu "Sadhu") -teacher [Shri Purohit Swami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shri_Purohit_Swami "Shri Purohit Swami") on their own translation of the Upanishads, eventually titled *[The Ten Principal Upanishads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Ten_Principal_Upanishads "The Ten Principal Upanishads")* and published in 1938. This translation was the final piece of work published by Yeats before his death less than a year later.[^209]

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/8a/Schopenhauer.jpg)

German 19th century philosopher Arthur Schopenhauer, impressed by the Upanishads, called the texts "the production of the highest human wisdom".

The German philosopher [Arthur Schopenhauer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Schopenhauer "Arthur Schopenhauer") read the Latin translation and praised the Upanishads in his main work, *[The World as Will and Representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_World_as_Will_and_Representation "The World as Will and Representation")* (1819), as well as in his *[Parerga and Paralipomena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parerga_and_Paralipomena "Parerga and Paralipomena")* (1851).[^210] He found his own philosophy in accord with the Upanishads, which taught that the individual is a manifestation of the one basis of reality. For Schopenhauer, that fundamentally real underlying unity is what we know in ourselves as "will". Schopenhauer used to keep a copy of the Latin *Oupnekhet* by his side and commented,

> In the whole world there is no study so beneficial and so elevating as that of the Upanishads. It has been the solace of my life, it will be the solace of my death.[^211]

Schopenhauer's philosophy influenced many famous people and introduced them to the Upanishads. One of them was the Austrian Physicist [Erwin Schrödinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erwin_Schr%C3%B6dinger "Erwin Schrödinger"), who once wrote:

> “There is obviously only one alternative,” he wrote, “namely the unification of minds or consciousnesses. Their multiplicity is only apparent, in truth there is only one mind. This is the doctrine of the Upanishads.” [^212]

Another German philosopher, [Friedrich Wilhelm Joseph Schelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Wilhelm_Joseph_Schelling "Friedrich Wilhelm Joseph Schelling"), praised the ideas in the Upanishads,[^213] as did others.[^214] In the United States, the group known as the [Transcendentalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendentalism "Transcendentalism") were influenced by the German idealists. Americans, such as [Emerson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Waldo_Emerson "Ralph Waldo Emerson") and [Thoreau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_David_Thoreau "Henry David Thoreau") embraced Schelling's interpretation of [Kant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Kant "Immanuel Kant") 's [Transcendental idealism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendental_idealism "Transcendental idealism"), as well as his celebration of the romantic, exotic, mystical aspect of the Upanishads. As a result of the influence of these writers, the Upanishads gained renown in Western countries.[^215]

The poet [T. S. Eliot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T._S._Eliot "T. S. Eliot"), inspired by his reading of the Upanishads, based the final portion of his famous poem *[The Waste Land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Waste_Land "The Waste Land")* (1922) upon one of its verses.[^216] According to [Eknath Easwaran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eknath_Easwaran "Eknath Easwaran"), the Upanishads are snapshots of towering peaks of consciousness.[^217]

[Juan Mascaró](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juan_Mascar%C3%B3 "Juan Mascaró"), a professor at the University of Barcelona and a translator of the Upanishads, states that the Upanishads represents for the Hindu approximately what the [New Testament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Testament "New Testament") represents for the Christian, and that the message of the Upanishads can be summarized in the words, "the kingdom of God is within you".[^218]

[Paul Deussen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Deussen "Paul Deussen") in his review of the Upanishads, states that the texts emphasize Brahman-Atman as something that can be experienced, but not defined.[^219] This view of the soul and self are similar, states Deussen, to those found in the dialogues of Plato and elsewhere. The Upanishads insisted on oneness of soul, excluded all plurality, and therefore, all proximity in space, all succession in time, all interdependence as cause and effect, and all opposition as subject and object.[^219] Max Müller, in his review of the Upanishads, summarizes the lack of systematic philosophy and the central theme in the Upanishads as follows,

> There is not what could be called a philosophical system in these Upanishads. They are, in the true sense of the word, guesses at truth, frequently contradicting each other, yet all tending in one direction. The key-note of the old Upanishads is "know thyself," but with a much deeper meaning than that of the *γνῶθι σεαυτόν* of the [Delphic Oracle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Know_thyself "Know thyself"). The "know thyself" of the Upanishads means, know thy true self, that which underlines thine Ego, and find it and know it in the highest, the eternal Self, the One without a second, which underlies the whole world.

—  [Max Müller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_M%C3%BCller "Max Müller")

The English composer [Gustav Holst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustav_Holst "Gustav Holst") based several works on Ancient Indian texts, including *[Hymns from the Rig Veda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hymns_from_the_Rig_Veda "Hymns from the Rig Veda")*, *[The Cloud Messenger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Cloud_Messenger_\(music\) "The Cloud Messenger (music)")* and the opera *[Sāvitri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C4%81vitri_\(opera\) "Sāvitri (opera)")*. The Upanishads was one of a small number of books that Holst kept with him for his whole life.[^220]
